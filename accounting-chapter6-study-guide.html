Learning Objective 1: Classifying and Determining Inventory

1.1 Classifying Inventory

Inventory classification differs between merchandising and manufacturing companies:

Merchandising Company

  • One Classification: Inventory
  • Consists of goods purchased for resale
  • Ready for sale to customers

Manufacturing Company

  • Three Classifications:
  • Raw Materials: Basic materials that will be used in production
  • Work in Process: Goods that are partially completed
  • Finished Goods: Completed products ready for sale
📌 Important Note: Regardless of classification, all inventories are reported under Current Assets on the statement of financial position.

1.2 Determining Inventory Quantities

Physical Inventory - Two Reasons for Taking:

Perpetual System:

  1. Check accuracy of inventory records
  2. Determine amount of inventory lost due to wasted raw materials, shoplifting, or employee theft

Periodic System:

  1. Determine the inventory on hand
  2. Determine the cost of goods sold for the period

Taking a Physical Inventory

  • Involves counting, weighing, or measuring each kind of inventory on hand
  • Companies often take inventory when:
    • Business is closed or business is slow
    • At the end of the accounting period
🔍 Ethics Note: In a famous fraud case, a salad oil company filled its storage tanks mostly with water. The oil rose to the top, so auditors thought the tanks were full of oil. The company also claimed it had more tanks than it really did by repainting numbers on the tanks to confuse auditors.

1.3 Determining Ownership of Goods

Goods in Transit

Key Principle: Goods in transit should be included in the inventory of the company that has legal title to the goods. Legal title is determined by the terms of sale.

FOB Shipping Point

  • Buyer pays freight costs
  • Ownership passes to buyer when the public carrier accepts the goods from the seller
  • Buyer includes goods in inventory while in transit

FOB Destination

  • Seller pays freight costs
  • Ownership remains with seller until goods reach the buyer
  • Seller includes goods in inventory while in transit
📦 Example: If Company A ships goods FOB shipping point on December 30, and the goods arrive at Company B on January 3, Company B should include these goods in its December 31 inventory count because ownership transferred when the carrier picked up the goods.

Consigned Goods

  • Consignment: To hold goods of other parties and try to sell them for a fee, without taking ownership
  • Many car, boat, and antique dealers sell goods on consignment
  • The consignor (owner) includes consigned goods in inventory
  • The consignee (holder) does NOT include consigned goods in inventory
⚠️ Common Error: Companies must be careful not to include consigned goods held for others in their inventory count, and must remember to include goods they own that are held on consignment by others.

Learning Objective 2: Inventory Costing Methods

2.1 Inventory Cost

Inventory is accounted for at cost.

  • Cost includes all expenditures necessary to acquire goods and place them in a condition ready for sale
  • Unit costs are applied to quantities to compute total cost of inventory and cost of goods sold

2.2 Cost Flow Assumptions

There are three inventory costing methods:

  1. Specific Identification
  2. First-In, First-Out (FIFO)
  3. Average-Cost
📌 Important: Cost flow does NOT need to be consistent with the physical movement of goods. These are cost flow assumptions, not physical flow requirements.

2.3 Specific Identification

  • Actual physical flow costing method
  • Items still in inventory are specifically costed to arrive at total cost of ending inventory
  • Practice is relatively rare
  • Used for high-value, low-volume items (cars, jewelry, art)
📺 Example: Crivitz TV purchases three identical 50-inch TVs:
  • February 3: 1 TV at £700
  • March 5: 1 TV at £750
  • May 22: 1 TV at £800
If Crivitz sold the TVs purchased on February 3 and May 22, then:
  • Cost of Goods Sold = £700 + £800 = £1,500
  • Ending Inventory = £750
⚠️ Ethics Note: A major disadvantage of specific identification is that management may be able to manipulate net income by choosing which units to sell (high-cost units to reduce income, or low-cost units to increase income).

2.4 First-In, First-Out (FIFO)

  • Costs of the earliest goods purchased are the first to be recognized in determining cost of goods sold
  • Often parallels actual physical flow of merchandise
  • Ending inventory is determined by taking the unit cost of the most recent purchase and working backward
FIFO Method - Step by Step:
Step 1: Calculate Ending Inventory
  • Start with most recent purchases
  • Work backward until all ending inventory units are costed
Step 2: Calculate Cost of Goods Sold
  • COGS = Cost of Goods Available for Sale - Ending Inventory
📊 Example - Lin Electronics (FIFO):
Data:
  • Jan 1: Beginning Inventory - 10 units @ HK$100 = HK$1,000
  • Apr 15: Purchase - 20 units @ HK$110 = HK$2,200
  • Aug 24: Purchase - 30 units @ HK$120 = HK$3,600
  • Nov 27: Purchase - 40 units @ HK$130 = HK$5,200
  • Total Available: 100 units = HK$12,000
  • Units Sold: 55 units
  • Ending Inventory: 45 units
Step 1 - Ending Inventory (45 units):
  • 40 units @ HK$130 = HK$5,200 (from Nov 27)
  • 5 units @ HK$120 = HK$600 (from Aug 24)
  • Total Ending Inventory = HK$5,800
Step 2 - Cost of Goods Sold:
  • HK$12,000 - HK$5,800 = HK$6,200
💡 Helpful Hint: Another way of thinking about FIFO ending inventory is the LISH assumption - Last In Still Here.

2.5 Average-Cost Method

  • Allocates cost of goods available for sale on the basis of weighted-average unit cost
  • Applies weighted-average unit cost to units on hand to determine ending inventory
Weighted-Average Unit Cost Formula:

Weighted-Average Unit Cost = Cost of Goods Available for Sale ÷ Total Units Available for Sale
📊 Example - Lin Electronics (Average-Cost):
Using same data as FIFO example: Step 1 - Calculate Weighted-Average Unit Cost:
  • HK$12,000 ÷ 100 units = HK$120 per unit
Step 2 - Ending Inventory (45 units):
  • 45 units × HK$120 = HK$5,400
Step 3 - Cost of Goods Sold:
  • HK$12,000 - HK$5,400 = HK$6,600
Alternative Calculation for COGS:
  • 55 units sold × HK$120 = HK$6,600

Learning Objective 3: Financial Effects of Cost Flow Assumptions

3.1 Acceptability of Methods

Either of the two cost flow assumptions (FIFO or Average-Cost) is acceptable for use under IFRS.

  • Examples:
    • adidas (DEU) and Lenovo (CHN) use average-cost
    • Syngenta Group (CHE) and Nokia (FIN) use FIFO
  • Survey Results:
    • ~60% use average-cost method
    • ~40% use FIFO
    • ~23% use both for different parts of inventory

3.2 Income Statement Effects

📊 Comparative Example - Lin Electronics:
Assuming Sales Revenue = HK$11,500 and Operating Expenses = HK$2,000
FIFO Average-Cost
Sales Revenue HK$11,500 HK$11,500
Cost of Goods Sold 6,200 6,600
Gross Profit 5,300 4,900
Operating Expenses 2,000 2,000
Net Income HK$3,300 HK$2,900

3.3 Statement of Financial Position Effects

During Periods of Inflation:

  • FIFO Advantage: Costs allocated to ending inventory approximate current cost
  • Average-Cost Shortcoming: Costs allocated to ending inventory may be understated in terms of current cost
📊 Comparison:
  • FIFO Ending Inventory: HK$5,800 (includes most recent, higher costs)
  • Average-Cost Ending Inventory: HK$5,400 (averages old and new costs)

3.4 Tax Effects

During Periods of Rising Prices:

  • FIFO:
    • Higher ending inventory
    • Lower cost of goods sold
    • Higher net income
    • Higher income taxes
  • Average-Cost:
    • Lower ending inventory
    • Higher cost of goods sold
    • Lower net income
    • Lower income taxes
🌍 Global Insight - LIFO: In the United States, companies can use LIFO (Last-In, First-Out), which is NOT allowed under IFRS. This makes comparing U.S. companies (like ExxonMobil) with international companies (like BP or Royal Dutch Shell) difficult. LIFO typically results in even lower taxes during inflation than average-cost.

3.5 Using Cost Flow Methods Consistently

  • Method should be used consistently to enhance comparability
  • Companies may change their inventory costing method, but must:
    • Disclose the change
    • Show the effect on financial statements
    • Justify the change

Learning Objective 4: Lower-of-Cost-or-Net Realizable Value (LCNRV)

4.1 The LCNRV Rule

When the value of inventory is lower than its cost, companies must "write down" the inventory to its net realizable value.

Net Realizable Value (NRV):
The amount that a company expects to realize (receive) from the sale of inventory.

4.2 Applying LCNRV

📊 Example - Gao TV Company:
Product Units Cost/Unit NRV/Unit LCNRV
Flat-screen TVs 100 NT$600 NT$550 NT$55,000
Satellite radios 500 90 104 45,000
DVD recorders 850 50 48 40,800
DVDs 3,000 5 6 15,000
Total Inventory NT$155,800

Explanation:

  • Flat-screen TVs: NRV (NT$550) < Cost (NT$600) → Use NT$550 × 100 = NT$55,000
  • Satellite radios: Cost (NT$90) < NRV (NT$104) → Use NT$90 × 500 = NT$45,000
  • DVD recorders: NRV (NT$48) < Cost (NT$50) → Use NT$48 × 850 = NT$40,800
  • DVDs: Cost (NT$5) < NRV (NT$6) → Use NT$5 × 3,000 = NT$15,000

Learning Objective 5: Inventory Errors

5.1 Common Causes of Inventory Errors

  • Failure to count or price inventory correctly
  • Not properly recognizing the transfer of legal title to goods in transit
  • Errors affect both the income statement and statement of financial position

5.2 Income Statement Effects

Cost of Goods Sold Formula:

Beginning Inventory + Cost of Goods Purchased - Ending Inventory = Cost of Goods Sold

Effects of Inventory Errors on Current Year:

When Inventory Error: COGS Is: Net Income Is:
Understates beginning inventory Understated Overstated
Overstates beginning inventory Overstated Understated
Understates ending inventory Overstated Understated
Overstates ending inventory Understated Overstated

5.3 Two-Period Effect

Key Principle: An error in ending inventory of the current period will have a reverse effect on net income of the next accounting period.

📊 Example - Two-Year Impact:
Assume ending inventory is understated by €3,000 in 2016:

2016 Effects:

  • Ending Inventory: Understated by €3,000
  • Cost of Goods Sold: Overstated by €3,000
  • Net Income: Understated by €3,000

2017 Effects (because 2016 ending = 2017 beginning):

  • Beginning Inventory: Understated by €3,000
  • Cost of Goods Sold: Understated by €3,000
  • Net Income: Overstated by €3,000

Combined Effect:

  • Over the two years, total net income is correct because the errors offset each other

5.4 Statement of Financial Position Effects

Basic Accounting Equation:
Assets = Liabilities + Equity
Ending Inventory Error Assets Liabilities Equity
Overstated Overstated No effect Overstated
Understated Understated No effect Understated

Learning Objective 6: Statement Presentation and Analysis

6.1 Presentation

Statement of Financial Position

  • Inventory is classified as a current asset

Income Statement

  • Cost of goods sold is subtracted from sales

Required Disclosures

  1. Major inventory classifications (raw materials, work in process, finished goods)
  2. Basis of accounting (cost or LCNRV)
  3. Costing method (specific identification, FIFO, or average-cost)

6.2 Analysis - Inventory Management

Inventory management is a double-edged sword:

High Inventory Levels

  • Disadvantage: High carrying costs
    • Investment costs
    • Storage costs
    • Insurance costs
    • Obsolescence risk
    • Damage risk

Low Inventory Levels

  • Disadvantage: May lead to:
    • Stock-outs
    • Lost sales
    • Customer dissatisfaction

6.3 Inventory Turnover

Inventory Turnover Formula:

Inventory Turnover = Cost of Goods Sold ÷ Average Inventory

Where: Average Inventory = (Beginning Inventory + Ending Inventory) ÷ 2

Interpretation: Measures the number of times on average the inventory is sold during the period.

6.4 Days in Inventory

Days in Inventory Formula:

Days in Inventory = 365 Days ÷ Inventory Turnover

Interpretation: Measures the average number of days inventory is held.

📊 Example - Esprit Holdings:
  • Beginning Inventory: HK$3,209 million
  • Ending Inventory: HK$3,254 million
  • Cost of Goods Sold: HK$12,071 million

Step 1 - Calculate Average Inventory:

  • Average Inventory = (HK$3,209 + HK$3,254) ÷ 2 = HK$3,231.5 million

Step 2 - Calculate Inventory Turnover:

  • Inventory Turnover = HK$12,071 ÷ HK$3,231.5 = 3.7 times

Step 3 - Calculate Days in Inventory:

  • Days in Inventory = 365 ÷ 3.7 = 99 days

Interpretation: Esprit Holdings sells its entire inventory approximately 3.7 times per year, or it takes about 99 days to sell its inventory.

💡 Analysis Tips:
  • Higher turnover generally indicates efficient inventory management
  • Lower days in inventory means faster-moving inventory
  • Compare ratios to:
    • Industry averages
    • Competitors
    • Company's historical trends

Question 1: Which of the following is NOT a classification of inventory for a manufacturing company?

  • a) Raw materials
  • b) Merchandise inventory
  • c) Work in process
  • d) Finished goods
✓ Correct Answer: b) Merchandise inventory
Explanation: Merchandise inventory is used by merchandising companies. Manufacturing companies use three classifications: raw materials, work in process, and finished goods.

Question 2: Under a perpetual inventory system, a physical inventory count is taken to:

  • a) Determine the cost of goods sold for the period
  • b) Calculate the ending inventory balance
  • c) Verify that purchases were recorded correctly
  • d) Check the accuracy of inventory records and determine losses
✓ Correct Answer: d) Check the accuracy of inventory records and determine losses
Explanation: In a perpetual system, physical counts verify the accuracy of records and identify inventory lost to theft, damage, or waste.

Question 3: Goods in transit should be included in the inventory of the buyer when the terms of sale are:

  • a) FOB destination
  • b) Consignment basis
  • c) When goods reach the buyer's warehouse
  • d) FOB shipping point
✓ Correct Answer: d) FOB shipping point
Explanation: Under FOB shipping point, ownership transfers to the buyer when the carrier accepts the goods, so the buyer includes them in inventory while in transit.

Question 4: Which party includes consigned goods in their inventory?

  • a) The consignee who holds the goods
  • b) The consignor who owns the goods
  • c) Both the consignor and consignee
  • d) Neither party until the goods are sold
✓ Correct Answer: b) The consignor who owns the goods
Explanation: The consignor retains ownership and includes consigned goods in inventory. The consignee only holds them for sale.

Question 5: Inventory cost includes all of the following EXCEPT:

  • a) Purchase price of the goods
  • b) Freight costs to acquire the goods
  • c) Selling costs to market the goods
  • d) Costs to place goods in condition ready for sale
✓ Correct Answer: c) Selling costs to market the goods
Explanation: Inventory cost includes all expenditures to acquire and prepare goods for sale, but NOT selling costs which are period expenses.

Question 6: Which inventory costing method tracks the actual physical flow of goods?

  • a) Specific identification
  • b) FIFO (First-In, First-Out)
  • c) Average-cost
  • d) All methods track physical flow
✓ Correct Answer: a) Specific identification
Explanation: Specific identification is the only method that tracks actual physical units. FIFO and average-cost are cost flow assumptions.

Question 7: Under FIFO, ending inventory consists of:

  • a) The oldest costs
  • b) An average of all costs
  • c) The most recent costs
  • d) The lowest costs available
✓ Correct Answer: c) The most recent costs
Explanation: FIFO assumes oldest costs go to COGS first, leaving the most recent (newest) costs in ending inventory. This is the LISH (Last In Still Here) concept.

Question 8: During periods of rising prices, which method will result in the highest net income?

  • a) Average-cost
  • b) FIFO
  • c) Specific identification
  • d) All methods produce the same net income
✓ Correct Answer: b) FIFO
Explanation: FIFO assigns oldest (lowest) costs to COGS, resulting in lower COGS and higher net income during inflation.

Question 9: The weighted-average unit cost is calculated by dividing:

  • a) Total units available by cost of goods sold
  • b) Ending inventory by total units sold
  • c) Cost of goods available for sale by total units available
  • d) Beginning inventory by purchases during the period
✓ Correct Answer: c) Cost of goods available for sale by total units available
Explanation: Weighted-average unit cost = Total cost of goods available ÷ Total units available for sale.

Question 10: Which inventory method is NOT allowed under IFRS?

  • a) LIFO (Last-In, First-Out)
  • b) FIFO (First-In, First-Out)
  • c) Average-cost
  • d) Specific identification
✓ Correct Answer: a) LIFO (Last-In, First-Out)
Explanation: IFRS does not permit LIFO. Only FIFO, average-cost, and specific identification are allowed under IFRS.

Question 11: During periods of rising prices, which method results in the lowest income taxes?

  • a) FIFO
  • b) Specific identification
  • c) All methods result in the same taxes
  • d) Average-cost
✓ Correct Answer: d) Average-cost
Explanation: Average-cost results in higher COGS and lower net income during inflation, which means lower income taxes.

Question 12: Net realizable value is:

  • a) The original cost of inventory
  • b) The amount expected to be received from selling inventory
  • c) The replacement cost of inventory
  • d) The average of cost and selling price
✓ Correct Answer: b) The amount expected to be received from selling inventory
Explanation: NRV is the estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business, representing what the company expects to realize from the sale.

Question 13: When applying LCNRV, if the net realizable value is less than cost, the inventory should be written down to:

  • a) Zero
  • b) Original cost
  • c) Net realizable value
  • d) The average of cost and NRV
✓ Correct Answer: c) Net realizable value
Explanation: Under LCNRV, when NRV < Cost, inventory is written down to the lower amount (NRV).

Question 14: If ending inventory is understated, then:

  • a) Cost of goods sold is understated and net income is overstated
  • b) Cost of goods sold is overstated and net income is understated
  • c) Both COGS and net income are understated
  • d) Both COGS and net income are overstated
✓ Correct Answer: b) Cost of goods sold is overstated and net income is understated
Explanation: Lower ending inventory → Higher COGS → Lower net income (inverse relationship).

Question 15: An error in ending inventory in Year 1 will:

  • a) Have a reverse effect on Year 2 net income
  • b) Not affect Year 2 at all
  • c) Have the same effect on Year 2 net income
  • d) Only affect the statement of financial position
✓ Correct Answer: a) Have a reverse effect on Year 2 net income
Explanation: Year 1 ending inventory becomes Year 2 beginning inventory, causing an opposite effect on Year 2 net income.

Question 16: Inventory turnover is calculated by dividing:

  • a) Sales by average inventory
  • b) Average inventory by cost of goods sold
  • c) Ending inventory by beginning inventory
  • d) Cost of goods sold by average inventory
✓ Correct Answer: d) Cost of goods sold by average inventory
Explanation: Inventory Turnover = Cost of Goods Sold ÷ Average Inventory.

Question 17: Days in inventory measures:

  • a) How many days it takes to purchase inventory
  • b) The number of days in the accounting period
  • c) The average number of days inventory is held
  • d) How long it takes to pay for inventory
✓ Correct Answer: c) The average number of days inventory is held
Explanation: Days in Inventory = 365 ÷ Inventory Turnover, showing how long inventory sits before being sold.

Question 18: A higher inventory turnover ratio generally indicates:

  • a) Poor inventory management
  • b) Efficient inventory management
  • c) Excessive inventory levels
  • d) Inventory obsolescence
✓ Correct Answer: b) Efficient inventory management
Explanation: Higher turnover means inventory is selling faster, indicating efficient management and less capital tied up in inventory.

Question 19: Which of the following is a disadvantage of high inventory levels?

  • a) High carrying costs including storage and insurance
  • b) Increased risk of stock-outs
  • c) Lost sales opportunities
  • d) Customer dissatisfaction
✓ Correct Answer: a) High carrying costs including storage and insurance
Explanation: High inventory levels increase costs for storage, insurance, and risk of obsolescence. Options b, c, and d are disadvantages of LOW inventory.

Question 20: Under FIFO during inflation, ending inventory on the statement of financial position:

  • a) Is understated compared to current cost
  • b) Equals the average cost of all purchases
  • c) Approximates current cost
  • d) Is always lower than under average-cost
✓ Correct Answer: c) Approximates current cost
Explanation: FIFO assigns the most recent (and highest during inflation) costs to ending inventory, making it closer to current replacement cost.

Question 21: Companies must disclose all of the following about inventory EXCEPT:

  • a) Major inventory classifications
  • b) Basis of accounting (cost or LCNRV)
  • c) Costing method used
  • d) Names of suppliers
✓ Correct Answer: d) Names of suppliers
Explanation: Required disclosures include classifications, basis of accounting, and costing method. Supplier names are not required.

Question 22: If beginning inventory is overstated, then:

  • a) Net income is overstated
  • b) Net income is understated
  • c) Assets are understated
  • d) There is no effect on net income
✓ Correct Answer: b) Net income is understated
Explanation: Overstated beginning inventory → Overstated COGS → Understated net income.

Question 23: The specific identification method is most appropriate for:

  • a) High-value, low-volume items like jewelry or automobiles
  • b) Large quantities of identical low-cost items
  • c) Perishable goods with short shelf lives
  • d) All types of inventory equally
✓ Correct Answer: a) High-value, low-volume items like jewelry or automobiles
Explanation: Specific identification is practical only for items that can be individually tracked, typically high-value, unique items.

Question 24: Consistency in using inventory costing methods is important because:

  • a) It is required by law in all countries
  • b) It prevents companies from ever changing methods
  • c) It enhances comparability of financial statements over time
  • d) All methods produce identical results when used consistently
✓ Correct Answer: c) It enhances comparability of financial statements over time
Explanation: Consistency allows users to compare financial statements across periods. Companies can change methods but must disclose and justify the change.

Question 25: If ending inventory is overstated in Year 1, what is the effect on total net income over a two-year period (Year 1 and Year 2 combined)?

  • a) Total net income is overstated
  • b) Total net income is correct (no effect)
  • c) Total net income is understated
  • d) The effect depends on the inventory method used
✓ Correct Answer: b) Total net income is correct (no effect)
Explanation: Inventory errors self-correct over two periods. Year 1 overstatement is offset by Year 2 understatement, making total net income correct.

📊 Case Study Questions

Case Study 1: Inventory Costing Methods

Scenario: TechMart Electronics had the following inventory transactions for Model X laptops during March:

  • March 1: Beginning inventory - 50 units @ $800 each
  • March 10: Purchased 100 units @ $850 each
  • March 20: Purchased 150 units @ $900 each
  • March 25: Sold 220 units @ $1,200 each

Question: Calculate the ending inventory and cost of goods sold under both FIFO and Average-Cost methods. Which method would result in higher net income and why?

Solution:

FIFO Method:
Total units available: 50 + 100 + 150 = 300 units
Units sold: 220 units
Ending inventory: 80 units

Ending Inventory (80 units from most recent):
80 units @ $900 = $72,000

Cost of Goods Sold:
50 units @ $800 = $40,000
100 units @ $850 = $85,000
70 units @ $900 = $63,000
Total COGS = $188,000

Average-Cost Method:
Total cost: (50 × $800) + (100 × $850) + (150 × $900) = $260,000
Weighted-average cost: $260,000 ÷ 300 = $866.67 per unit

Ending Inventory: 80 × $866.67 = $69,334
Cost of Goods Sold: 220 × $866.67 = $190,667

Conclusion: FIFO results in higher net income ($264,000 - $188,000 = $76,000 gross profit) compared to Average-Cost ($264,000 - $190,667 = $73,333 gross profit) because FIFO assigns older, lower costs to COGS during this period of rising prices.

Case Study 2: Goods in Transit

Scenario: Global Imports is preparing its year-end financial statements for December 31, 2023. The company's physical inventory count shows $450,000 in the warehouse. The following additional information is available:

  • Goods costing $25,000 were shipped FOB shipping point on December 29 and are in transit. They will arrive January 3.
  • Goods costing $18,000 were shipped to a customer FOB destination on December 30 and are in transit.
  • Goods costing $12,000 are held on consignment from Supplier A.
  • Goods costing $8,000 owned by Global Imports are on consignment with Retailer B.

Question: What amount should Global Imports report as inventory on December 31, 2023?

Solution:

Physical count: $450,000

Adjustments:
1. FOB shipping point purchase (+$25,000): Ownership transferred when shipped, so Global Imports owns these goods
2. FOB destination sale (+$18,000): Ownership remains with Global Imports until goods reach customer
3. Consigned goods from Supplier A (-$12,000): Global Imports doesn't own these, should not be in inventory
4. Goods on consignment with Retailer B (+$8,000): Global Imports still owns these

Calculation:
$450,000 + $25,000 + $18,000 - $12,000 + $8,000 = $489,000

Correct Inventory Amount: $489,000

Case Study 3: Lower-of-Cost-or-Net Realizable Value

Scenario: Fashion Boutique has the following inventory at year-end:

Product Units Cost/Unit NRV/Unit
Dresses 200 $75 $85
Shoes 150 $60 $50
Handbags 100 $120 $110

Question: Calculate the total inventory value using LCNRV. What is the total write-down amount?

Solution:

Dresses: Cost ($75) < NRV ($85) → Use $75 × 200 = $15,000
Shoes: NRV ($50) < Cost ($60) → Use $50 × 150 = $7,500
Handbags: NRV ($110) < Cost ($120) → Use $110 × 100 = $11,000

Total Inventory Value (LCNRV): $33,500

Write-down Calculation:
Original cost: (200 × $75) + (150 × $60) + (100 × $120) = $36,000
LCNRV value: $33,500
Total Write-down: $2,500

This write-down consists of:
- Shoes: (150 × $10) = $1,500
- Handbags: (100 × $10) = $1,000

Case Study 4: Inventory Errors

Scenario: Precision Manufacturing discovered that its December 31, 2022 ending inventory was overstated by $45,000 due to a counting error. The company had reported the following for 2022 and 2023:

2022 (Reported) 2023 (Reported)
Sales $500,000 $550,000
Cost of Goods Sold $300,000 $330,000
Gross Profit $200,000 $220,000

Question: Calculate the corrected gross profit for both 2022 and 2023. What is the combined effect on total gross profit over the two years?

Solution:

2022 Analysis:
Ending inventory overstated by $45,000
→ COGS understated by $45,000
→ Gross profit overstated by $45,000

Corrected 2022 COGS: $300,000 + $45,000 = $345,000
Corrected 2022 Gross Profit: $500,000 - $345,000 = $155,000

2023 Analysis:
Beginning inventory (2022 ending) overstated by $45,000
→ COGS overstated by $45,000
→ Gross profit understated by $45,000

Corrected 2023 COGS: $330,000 - $45,000 = $285,000
Corrected 2023 Gross Profit: $550,000 - $285,000 = $265,000

Combined Effect:
Reported total: $200,000 + $220,000 = $420,000
Corrected total: $155,000 + $265,000 = $420,000
Difference: $0 (errors offset over two years)

Case Study 5: Inventory Analysis

Scenario: RetailCo and CompetitorX are both in the electronics retail industry. Their inventory information for 2023 is as follows:

RetailCo CompetitorX
Beginning Inventory $180,000 $250,000
Ending Inventory $220,000 $290,000
Cost of Goods Sold $1,600,000 $1,890,000

Question: Calculate inventory turnover and days in inventory for both companies. Which company manages its inventory more efficiently and why?

Solution:

RetailCo:
Average Inventory = ($180,000 + $220,000) ÷ 2 = $200,000
Inventory Turnover = $1,600,000 ÷ $200,000 = 8.0 times
Days in Inventory = 365 ÷ 8.0 = 45.6 days

CompetitorX:
Average Inventory = ($250,000 + $290,000) ÷ 2 = $270,000

Essay Question 3: Lower-of-Cost-or-Net Realizable Value

Question: Discuss the lower-of-cost-or-net realizable value (LCNRV) rule for inventory valuation. In your answer:

  • Explain what net realizable value means
  • Describe why the LCNRV rule is necessary and what accounting principle it supports
  • Explain how to apply the LCNRV rule
  • Discuss the financial statement effects of writing down inventory to NRV
Answer Guidelines:

Your essay should include:

1. Net Realizable Value Definition:
- Amount company expects to receive from selling inventory
- Estimated selling price in ordinary course of business
- Represents realizable value, not replacement cost

2. Why LCNRV is Necessary:
- Supports conservatism principle
- Prevents overstatement of assets
- Recognizes losses when they occur, not when inventory is sold
- Reflects economic reality when inventory value declines

3. Application:
- Compare cost to NRV for each inventory item
- Use lower amount for valuation
- If NRV < Cost, write down to NRV
- If Cost < NRV, keep at cost

4. Financial Statement Effects:
- Reduces inventory on statement of financial position
- Increases cost of goods sold (or creates loss)
- Decreases net income
- Decreases assets and equity
- Write-down is not reversed if value later increases

Essay Question 5: FOB Terms and Inventory Ownership

Question: Explain the importance of FOB (Free on Board) terms in determining inventory ownership. In your answer:

  • Define FOB Shipping Point and FOB Destination
  • Explain when ownership transfers under each term
  • Discuss who pays freight costs under each term
  • Explain why understanding FOB terms is critical for accurate financial reporting
  • Provide examples of how incorrect treatment of goods in transit can affect financial statements
Answer Guidelines:

Your essay should include:

1. FOB Shipping Point:
- Ownership transfers when carrier accepts goods from seller
- Buyer pays freight costs
- Buyer includes goods in inventory while in transit
- Buyer bears risk of loss during shipment

2. FOB Destination:
- Ownership transfers when goods reach buyer
- Seller pays freight costs
- Seller includes goods in inventory while in transit
- Seller bears risk of loss during shipment

3. Importance for Financial Reporting:
- Determines which company includes goods in inventory
- Affects accuracy of inventory counts
- Impacts cost of goods sold calculation
- Critical for year-end cutoff procedures
- Affects both buyer's and seller's financial statements

4. Examples of Incorrect Treatment:
- Buyer fails to include FOB shipping point purchases in transit → Understated inventory and assets
- Seller fails to include FOB destination sales in transit → Understated inventory
- Double counting if both parties include same goods
- Neither party includes goods → Inventory "disappears" from financial statements

5. Best Practices:
- Maintain detailed shipping records
- Review FOB terms on all purchase orders and sales invoices
- Perform careful year-end cutoff procedures
- Reconcile goods in transit at period end
- Communicate between purchasing and accounting departments
