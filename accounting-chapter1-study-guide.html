Chapter 1 - Financial Accounting IFRS 3rd Edition

What is Accounting?

Definition

Accounting consists of three basic activities that identify, record, and communicate the economic events of an organization to interested users.

The Three Basic Activities

1. Identifying

The first step in the accounting process involves selecting economic events (transactions) that are relevant to a particular business.

Example: When a company purchases computer equipment, this is an economic event that needs to be identified and recorded. However, hiring a new employee is not immediately recorded as a transaction (though their salary payments will be).

2. Recording

Once identified, economic events are recorded, classified, and summarized in a systematic manner. This process includes the bookkeeping function.

  • Recording: Entering transaction data in accounting records
  • Classifying: Grouping similar items together (e.g., all cash transactions)
  • Summarizing: Aggregating data into useful formats
Key Point: The accounting process includes the bookkeeping function, but accounting is much broader than just bookkeeping. Accountants also analyze and interpret financial information.

3. Communicating

The final step involves preparing accounting reports and analyzing and interpreting them for users. This communication happens through financial statements.

  • Prepare accounting reports (financial statements)
  • Analyze financial information
  • Interpret results for decision-makers

Users and Uses of Accounting Data

Internal Users

Internal users are managers and employees who plan, organize, and run the business. They use accounting information to make decisions about company operations.

Types of Internal Users and Their Questions:

  1. Marketing: "What price for a Nokia cell phone will maximize the company's net income?"
  2. Management: "Which PepsiCo product line is the most profitable? Should any product lines be eliminated?"
  3. Finance: "Is cash sufficient to pay dividends to SAP shareholders?"
  4. Human Resources: "Can we afford to give Toyota employees pay raises this year?"

External Users

External users are individuals and organizations outside the company who need financial information about the company.

Types of External Users and Their Questions:

1. Investors

Use accounting information to decide whether to buy, hold, or sell shares.

  • "Is Royal Dutch Shell earning satisfactory income?"
  • "How does Disney compare in size and profitability with Time Warner?"

2. Creditors

Use accounting information to evaluate the risks of lending money.

  • "Will Singapore Airlines be able to pay its debts as they come due?"
Important Distinction: Internal users need detailed, frequent information for day-to-day operations. External users typically receive summarized information at regular intervals (monthly, quarterly, annually).

Ethics in Financial Reporting

Definition of Ethics

Ethics are the standards of conduct by which one's actions are judged as right or wrong, honest or dishonest, fair or not fair.

Why Ethics Matter in Accounting

Effective financial reporting depends on sound ethical behavior. Without ethical practices, financial statements lose their credibility and usefulness.

Recent Financial Scandals Include:

  • Enron (USA) - Accounting fraud and corporate corruption
  • Parmalat (ITA) - Europe's largest bankruptcy due to fraud
  • Satyam Computer Services (IND) - Massive accounting fraud
  • AIG (USA) - Financial crisis involvement
  • Garuda Indonesia (2018) - Financial reporting irregularities
  • e-fishery (2024) - Recent accounting issues

Steps in Analyzing Ethics Cases

Step 1: Recognize an Ethical Situation and the Ethical Issues Involved

  • Use your personal ethics to identify ethical situations and issues
  • Some businesses and professional organizations provide written codes of ethics for guidance
  • Be aware that ethical dilemmas exist in business situations

Step 2: Identify and Analyze the Principal Elements in the Situation

  • Identify the stakeholders - persons or groups who may be harmed or benefited
  • Ask: "What are the responsibilities and obligations of the parties involved?"
  • Consider all parties who might be affected by the decision

Step 3: Identify the Alternatives and Weigh the Impact of Each Alternative on Various Stakeholders

  • Select the most ethical alternative, considering all the consequences
  • Sometimes there will be one right answer
  • Other situations involve more than one right solution
  • These situations require an evaluation of each alternative and selection of the best one

Case Study: Dewey & LeBoeuf LLP

Situation: Employees of the law firm helped overstate revenue and use accounting tricks to hide losses and cover up cash shortages.

Employee Comments:

  • "I was instructed by the CFO to create invoices, knowing they would not be sent to clients. When I created these invoices, I knew that it was inappropriate."
  • "I intentionally gave the auditors incorrect information in the course of the audit."

Analysis: A small group of lower-level employees over a period of years carried out the instructions of their bosses. Their bosses seemed to have no concern, referring to their financial manipulations as "accounting tricks," "cooking the books," and "fake income."

Ethical Issue: Employees felt pressure to comply with unethical requests from superiors, compromising their professional integrity.

Accounting Standards and Measurement Principles

Two Major Accounting Standard-Setting Bodies

1. International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)

  • Website: http://www.iasb.org/
  • Standards: International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS)
  • Usage: Used by more than 130 countries worldwide
  • Scope: Global accounting standards

2. Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)

  • Website: http://www.fasb.org/
  • Standards: Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)
  • Usage: Used almost exclusively in the USA
  • Scope: U.S. accounting standards
Key Point: This textbook focuses on IFRS (International Financial Reporting Standards) as they are used globally. However, understanding both IFRS and GAAP is valuable for international business.

Measurement Principles

1. Historical Cost Principle (Cost Principle)

Dictates that companies record assets at their cost - the amount paid to acquire them.

Example: If company ABC buys an office for 2 Milyar (2 billion rupiah) in 1990, it will still be recorded as 2 Milyar in 2024, even though the market value may have changed significantly.

Advantages:

  • Reliable and verifiable
  • Based on actual transactions
  • Objective measurement

Disadvantages:

  • May not reflect current market value
  • Can become outdated over time

2. Fair Value Principle

States that assets and liabilities should be reported at fair value - the price received to sell an asset or settle a liability.

Fair value is:

  • The price that would be received to sell an asset
  • The price that would be paid to settle a liability
  • Based on current market conditions

Helpful Hint

Relevance and faithful representation are two primary qualities that make accounting information useful for decision-making.

  • Relevance: Information that can influence decisions
  • Faithful Representation: Information that accurately depicts what it claims to represent

Accounting Assumptions

1. Monetary Unit Assumption

Requires that companies include in the accounting records only transaction data that can be expressed in terms of money.

What Gets Recorded:

  • ✓ Purchase of equipment for $10,000
  • ✓ Payment of salaries totaling €50,000
  • ✓ Sale of goods for £25,000

What Does NOT Get Recorded:

  • ✗ Employee morale
  • ✗ Customer satisfaction
  • ✗ Quality of management
  • ✗ Brand reputation

Reason: These items, while important, cannot be reliably measured in monetary terms.

2. Economic Entity Assumption

Requires that activities of the entity be kept separate and distinct from the activities of its owner and all other economic entities.

Key Principle: The business is separate from its owners. Personal transactions of owners should not be mixed with business transactions.

Forms of Business Ownership

The economic entity assumption applies to all forms of business ownership:

1. Proprietorship

  • Ownership: Owned by one person
  • Management: Owner is often manager/operator
  • Profits/Losses: Owner receives any profits, suffers any losses
  • Liability: Owner is personally liable for all debts
  • Examples: Small retail stores, service businesses, consultants

2. Partnership

  • Ownership: Owned by two or more persons
  • Common Types: Often retail and service-type businesses
  • Liability: Generally unlimited personal liability for all partners
  • Agreement: Governed by partnership agreement
  • Examples: Law firms, accounting firms, medical practices

3. Corporation

  • Ownership: Ownership divided into shares of stock
  • Legal Status: Separate legal entity organized under corporation law
  • Liability: Limited liability for shareholders
  • Advantages: Easy to transfer ownership, can raise capital more easily
  • Examples: Most large businesses (Apple, Toyota, Samsung)

Economic Entity Assumption in Practice:

If the owner of a proprietorship buys a car:

  • ✓ If purchased for business use → Record in business accounts
  • ✗ If purchased for personal use → Do NOT record in business accounts

The Basic Accounting Equation

The Fundamental Equation

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

This equation provides the underlying framework for recording and summarizing economic events. The two sides of the equation must always be equal.

Components of the Equation

1. Assets

Definition: Resources a business owns that provide future services or benefits.

Examples of Assets:

  • Cash: Money in bank accounts and on hand
  • Accounts Receivable: Money owed to the company by customers
  • Inventory: Goods held for sale
  • Supplies: Items used in operations (office supplies, etc.)
  • Equipment: Machinery, computers, vehicles
  • Buildings: Structures owned by the company
  • Land: Property owned by the company

2. Liabilities

Definition: Claims against assets - debts and obligations of the business.

Creditors: The party to whom money is owed.

Examples of Liabilities:

  • Accounts Payable: Money owed to suppliers
  • Notes Payable: Formal written promises to pay
  • Salaries and Wages Payable: Money owed to employees
  • Interest Payable: Interest owed on loans
  • Taxes Payable: Taxes owed to government

3. Equity

Definition: The ownership claim on total assets. Also referred to as residual equity.

Components of Equity:

  • Share Capital—Ordinary: Investments by shareholders (amount paid for shares)
  • Retained Earnings: Cumulative net income retained in the business

Expanded Accounting Equation:

Assets = Liabilities + Share Capital + Retained Earnings

Retained Earnings = Revenues - Expenses - Dividends

Changes in Equity

Increases in Equity:

  1. Investments by Shareholders: When shareholders purchase shares, equity increases
  2. Revenues: Result from business activities entered into for the purpose of earning income

Decreases in Equity:

  1. Dividends: Distribution of cash or other assets to shareholders
  2. Expenses: The cost of assets consumed or services used in earning revenue

Common Sources of Revenue:

  • Sales revenue (from selling goods)
  • Service revenue (from providing services)
  • Fees earned
  • Commissions
  • Interest revenue
  • Dividend revenue
  • Royalties
  • Rent revenue

Common Types of Expenses:

  • Salaries and wages expense
  • Rent expense
  • Utilities expense (electricity, water, gas)
  • Property tax expense
  • Advertising expense
  • Interest expense
  • Insurance expense
  • Supplies expense
Important Note: Dividends are NOT expenses. Dividends are distributions of retained earnings to shareholders and are shown as a reduction of equity, not as an expense on the income statement.

Analyzing Business Transactions

What is a Transaction?

Transactions are a business's economic events recorded by accountants.

Characteristics of Transactions:

  • May be external (with outside parties) or internal (within the company)
  • Not all activities represent transactions
  • Each transaction has a dual effect on the accounting equation
  • Must be measurable in monetary terms

Transaction Identification Process:

Event Record? Reason
Purchase computer ✓ Yes Changes financial position (assets)
Discuss product design with customer ✗ No No change in financial position yet
Pay rent ✓ Yes Changes financial position (assets and equity)

Rules for Transaction Analysis

  1. Each transaction must be analyzed in terms of its effect on:
    • The three components of the basic accounting equation
    • Specific types (kinds) of items within each component
  2. The two sides of the equation must always be equal
  3. The Share Capital and Retained Earnings columns indicate the causes of each change in shareholders' equity

Example: Softbyte SA Transactions

Transaction 1: Investment by Shareholders

Event: Ray and Barbara Neal invest €15,000 cash in exchange for ordinary shares of Softbyte SA.

Analysis:

  • Assets (Cash) increase by €15,000
  • Equity (Share Capital) increases by €15,000

Equation: Assets (+€15,000) = Liabilities (0) + Equity (+€15,000)

Transaction 2: Purchase of Equipment for Cash

Event: Softbyte SA purchases computer equipment for €7,000 cash.

Analysis:

  • Assets (Cash) decrease by €7,000
  • Assets (Equipment) increase by €7,000
  • Total assets remain unchanged

Equation: Assets (-€7,000 + €7,000 = 0) = Liabilities (0) + Equity (0)

Transaction 3: Purchase of Supplies on Credit

Event: Softbyte SA purchases supplies for €1,600 on credit (to be paid later).

Analysis:

  • Assets (Supplies) increase by €1,600
  • Liabilities (Accounts Payable) increase by €1,600

Equation: Assets (+€1,600) = Liabilities (+€1,600) + Equity (0)

Transaction 4: Services Performed for Cash

Event: Softbyte SA receives €1,200 cash from customers for services performed.

Analysis:

  • Assets (Cash) increase by €1,200
  • Equity (Retained Earnings - Revenue) increases by €1,200

Equation: Assets (+€1,200) = Liabilities (0) + Equity (+€1,200 Revenue)

Transaction 5: Purchase of Advertising on Credit

Event: Softbyte SA receives a bill for €250 for advertising but postpones payment.

Analysis:

  • Liabilities (Accounts Payable) increase by €250
  • Equity (Retained Earnings - Expense) decreases by €250

Equation: Assets (0) = Liabilities (+€250) + Equity (-€250 Expense)

Financial Statements

Companies prepare five financial statements from the accounting data:

  1. Income Statement
  2. Retained Earnings Statement
  3. Statement of Financial Position (Balance Sheet)
  4. Statement of Cash Flows
  5. Comprehensive Income Statement

1. Income Statement

Purpose:

Reports the profitability of the company's operations over a specific period of time.

Key Features:

  • Lists revenues first, followed by expenses
  • Shows net income (when revenues > expenses) or net loss (when expenses > revenues)
  • Does NOT include investment and dividend transactions between shareholders and the business
  • Covers a period of time (e.g., "For the Month Ended September 30, 2017")

Formula:

Net Income = Revenues - Expenses

2. Retained Earnings Statement

Purpose:

Reports the changes in retained earnings for a specific period of time.

Key Features:

  • The time period is the same as that covered by the income statement
  • Shows why retained earnings increased or decreased during the period
  • Connects the income statement to the statement of financial position

Formula:

Ending Retained Earnings = Beginning Retained Earnings + Net Income - Dividends

3. Statement of Financial Position (Balance Sheet)

Purpose:

Reports the assets, liabilities, and equity at a specific date.

Key Features:

  • Lists assets at the top, followed by liabilities and equity
  • Total assets must equal total liabilities and equity
  • Is a snapshot of the company's financial condition at a specific moment in time
  • Usually prepared at month-end or year-end

4. Statement of Cash Flows

Purpose:

Provides information about the cash receipts and cash payments of a company during a period.

Three Categories of Cash Flows:

1. Operating Activities

Cash flows from operating activities are the cash effects of transactions that create revenues and expenses.

  • Cash received from customers
  • Cash paid to suppliers
  • Cash paid for operating expenses
  • Cash paid for interest and taxes

2. Investing Activities

Cash flows from investing activities include making and collecting loans and acquiring and disposing of investments and property, plant, and equipment.

  • Purchase or sale of equipment
  • Purchase or sale of buildings
  • Purchase or sale of investments

3. Financing Activities

Cash flows from financing activities include obtaining cash from issuing debt and repaying amounts borrowed, and obtaining cash from shareholders and providing them with a return on their investment.

  • Issuance of shares
  • Payment of dividends
  • Borrowing money
  • Repayment of loans

5. Comprehensive Income Statement

Purpose:

Reports all changes in equity during a period except those resulting from investments by shareholders and distributions to shareholders.

Components:

  • Net Income: From the income statement
  • Other Comprehensive Income: Items that bypass the income statement but affect equity

Interrelationships of Financial Statements

The financial statements are interconnected:

  1. Income Statement → Net income flows to → Retained Earnings Statement
  2. Retained Earnings Statement → Ending retained earnings flows to → Statement of Financial Position
  3. Statement of Cash Flows → Ending cash balance equals cash on → Statement of Financial Position

📊 Accounting Across the Organization

Reliable Financial Reporting: A Matter of Trust

The collapse of Enron, WorldCom, and other companies has made investors very concerned about the accuracy of financial statements. Regulators and lawmakers have responded by taking steps to improve the quality and reliability of financial reporting.

Key Insight: Effective financial reporting depends on sound ethical behavior. Users of financial statements must have confidence that the information is reliable and prepared according to accepted standards.

Case Study Question 16:

Scenario: TechStart SA is a new software development company. On January 1, 2024, the following transactions occurred:
- The founders invested €50,000 cash in exchange for ordinary shares
- The company purchased computer equipment for €20,000 cash
- The company purchased office supplies for €3,000 on credit
- The company paid €2,000 cash for January rent

Question: What is TechStart SA's total assets at the end of January 1, 2024?
A) €50,000 representing only the initial cash investment
B) €51,000 including cash, equipment, and supplies
C) €48,000 after deducting rent and credit purchases
D) €71,000 including all transactions without adjustments
Correct Answer: B (€51,000)
Analysis:
Cash: €50,000 - €20,000 - €2,000 = €28,000
Equipment: €20,000
Supplies: €3,000
Total Assets = €28,000 + €20,000 + €3,000 = €51,000
The rent payment is an expense that reduces equity, not assets. The supplies purchase on credit increases both assets and liabilities.

Case Study Question 18:

Scenario: RetailCo SA reported the following for September 2024:
- Service revenue: €45,000
- Salaries expense: €18,000
- Rent expense: €5,000
- Utilities expense: €2,000
- Dividends paid: €8,000
- Beginning retained earnings: €30,000

Question: What is RetailCo SA's ending retained earnings for September 2024?
A) €42,000 calculated by adding net income to beginning balance
B) €42,000 after adding net income and subtracting dividends
C) €50,000 by adding revenues directly to retained earnings
D) €12,000 representing only the net income for the period
Correct Answer: B (€42,000)
Analysis:
Net Income = Revenues - Expenses = €45,000 - (€18,000 + €5,000 + €2,000) = €20,000
Ending Retained Earnings = Beginning RE + Net Income - Dividends
= €30,000 + €20,000 - €8,000 = €42,000
Remember: Dividends are NOT expenses; they are distributions of retained earnings to shareholders.
Case Study Question 19:

Scenario: DesignPro Partnership has the following account balances on December 31, 2024:
- Cash: €25,000
- Accounts Receivable: €15,000
- Equipment: €80,000
- Accounts Payable: €20,000
- Notes Payable: €40,000

The partners are considering whether to incorporate. They want to know their current equity position.

Question: What is the total equity of DesignPro Partnership?
A) €120,000 representing the sum of all asset accounts
B) €60,000 calculated using the accounting equation
C) €40,000 representing only the cash and receivables
D) €80,000 equal to the value of equipment owned
Correct Answer: B (€60,000)
Analysis:
Using the accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Equity
Total Assets = €25,000 + €15,000 + €80,000 = €120,000
Total Liabilities = €20,000 + €40,000 = €60,000
Equity = Assets - Liabilities = €120,000 - €60,000 = €60,000
This equity represents the partners' ownership claim on the business assets after all liabilities are paid.
Case Study Question 20:

Scenario: ConsultCo SA is preparing its Statement of Cash Flows for March 2024. The following cash transactions occurred:
- Received €60,000 cash from customers for services
- Paid €25,000 cash for employee salaries
- Purchased office equipment for €30,000 cash
- Received €50,000 cash from issuing new shares
- Paid €10,000 cash dividend to shareholders
- Paid €8,000 cash for rent and utilities

Question: What is the net cash provided by (or used in) operating activities?
A) €60,000 representing total cash received from customers
B) €35,000 after subtracting only salary expenses from revenue
C) €27,000 from operating cash receipts minus operating payments
D) €37,000 including all cash transactions for the period
Correct Answer: C (€27,000)
Analysis:
Operating Activities:
Cash from customers: +€60,000
Cash paid for salaries: -€25,000
Cash paid for rent/utilities: -€8,000
Net cash from operating activities = €60,000 - €25,000 - €8,000 = €27,000

Note: Equipment purchase (€30,000) is an investing activity. Share issuance (€50,000) and dividends (€10,000) are financing activities. These are NOT included in operating activities.
