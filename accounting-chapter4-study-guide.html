Section 1: Using a Worksheet

What is a Worksheet?

A worksheet is a multiple-column form used in the adjustment process and in preparing financial statements. It is an optional tool that accountants use to organize data and ensure accuracy before preparing formal financial statements.

Key Points:

  • Worksheets are NOT a permanent accounting record
  • They are NOT financial statements
  • They are a working tool used internally by accountants
  • Companies generally computerize worksheets using spreadsheet programs

Steps in Preparing a Worksheet

The worksheet preparation involves five steps that must be completed in order:

  1. Prepare a Trial Balance on the Worksheet

    Enter all ledger accounts with balances in the account titles column. Enter debit and credit amounts from the ledger in the trial balance columns. Prove the equality of the two columns (total debits = total credits).

  2. Enter the Adjustments in the Adjustments Columns

    Turn to the adjustment data and enter the adjustments in the adjustments columns. Use key letters to cross-reference debit and credit adjustments. Prove the equality of the adjustments columns (total debit adjustments = total credit adjustments).

  3. Enter Adjusted Balances in the Adjusted Trial Balance Columns

    Combine the amounts in the trial balance and adjustments columns, and enter them in the adjusted trial balance columns. For each line, add or subtract the adjustment amount from the trial balance amount. Prove the equality of the adjusted trial balance columns.

  4. Extend Adjusted Trial Balance Amounts to Appropriate Financial Statement Columns

    Transfer the amounts from the adjusted trial balance to either the income statement columns or the statement of financial position columns. Assets, liabilities, and equity accounts go to the statement of financial position columns. Revenue and expense accounts go to the income statement columns.

  5. Total the Statement Columns, Compute Profit or Loss, and Complete the Worksheet

    Total each of the financial statement columns. Calculate profit or loss as the difference between total revenues and total expenses. Enter the profit (or loss) in the appropriate columns to balance the worksheet. Prove the equality: total debits = total credits in both the income statement and statement of financial position columns.

Example: Worksheet Preparation

Pioneer Advertising Inc. - October 31 Worksheet

Trial Balance (Step 1):

Account Debit Credit
Cash $15,200
Supplies $2,500
Prepaid Insurance $600
Equipment $5,000
Accounts Payable $2,500
Share Capital—Ordinary $10,000
Service Revenue $10,000
Salaries and Wages Expense $4,000
Rent Expense $900

Adjustments (Step 2):

  • (a) Supplies used: $1,500
  • (b) Insurance expired: $50
  • (c) Depreciation on equipment: $40
  • (d) Accrued salaries: $1,200

Result (Step 5): After completing all steps, if total revenues ($10,000) exceed total expenses ($7,690), the difference is profit ($2,310). This amount is entered in the income statement debit column and the statement of financial position credit column to balance the worksheet.

Preparing Financial Statements from a Worksheet

Once the worksheet is complete, financial statements can be prepared directly from it:

  • Income Statement: Prepared from the income statement columns of the worksheet
  • Retained Earnings Statement: Prepared from the retained earnings account in the statement of financial position columns, plus profit (or minus loss) from the income statement
  • Statement of Financial Position: Prepared from the statement of financial position columns of the worksheet

Important: The worksheet does NOT replace these formal financial statements. It is merely a tool to help prepare them.

Section 2: Closing the Books

Purpose of Closing Entries

Closing entries are journal entries made at the end of an accounting period to transfer the balances of temporary accounts to a permanent equity account (Retained Earnings).

Why Close the Books?

  • To prepare revenue, expense, and dividend accounts for the next period by reducing their balances to zero
  • To update the Retained Earnings account to its end-of-period balance
  • To separate financial data for each accounting period

Temporary vs. Permanent Accounts

Temporary Accounts (Nominal Accounts)

These accounts relate ONLY to a given accounting period and are closed at the end of each period:

  • All revenue accounts (e.g., Service Revenue, Sales Revenue)
  • All expense accounts (e.g., Salaries Expense, Rent Expense, Depreciation Expense)
  • Dividends (or Drawings for sole proprietorships)

Permanent Accounts (Real Accounts)

These accounts relate to one or more future accounting periods and are NOT closed:

  • All asset accounts (e.g., Cash, Accounts Receivable, Equipment)
  • All liability accounts (e.g., Accounts Payable, Notes Payable)
  • All equity accounts (e.g., Share Capital, Retained Earnings)

Permanent account balances are carried forward to the next accounting period.

The Income Summary Account

Income Summary is a temporary account used ONLY during the closing process. It:

  • Collects the balances of revenue and expense accounts
  • Shows the profit or loss for the period
  • Is then closed to Retained Earnings
  • Has a zero balance after the closing process is complete

Four Steps in the Closing Process

Closing entries are prepared in a specific order:

  1. Close Revenue Accounts to Income Summary

    Debit each revenue account for its balance and credit Income Summary for the total revenues.

    Dr. Service Revenue
        Cr. Income Summary

    Purpose: This entry transfers total revenues to Income Summary and reduces revenue accounts to zero.

  2. Close Expense Accounts to Income Summary

    Debit Income Summary for the total expenses and credit each expense account for its balance.

    Dr. Income Summary
        Cr. Salaries and Wages Expense
        Cr. Rent Expense
        Cr. Depreciation Expense
        Cr. (all other expense accounts)

    Purpose: This entry transfers total expenses to Income Summary and reduces expense accounts to zero.

  3. Close Income Summary to Retained Earnings

    If there is profit (credit balance in Income Summary):

    Dr. Income Summary
        Cr. Retained Earnings

    If there is a loss (debit balance in Income Summary):

    Dr. Retained Earnings
        Cr. Income Summary

    Purpose: This entry transfers profit or loss to Retained Earnings and reduces Income Summary to zero.

  4. Close Dividends to Retained Earnings

    Debit Retained Earnings and credit Dividends for the balance in the Dividends account.

    Dr. Retained Earnings
        Cr. Dividends

    Purpose: This entry transfers dividends to Retained Earnings and reduces the Dividends account to zero.

    Note: Dividends are NOT an expense and are NOT closed to Income Summary. They are a distribution of profit and are closed directly to Retained Earnings.

Example: Closing Entries

Pioneer Advertising Inc. - October 31 Closing Entries

Given Information:

  • Service Revenue: $10,600
  • Salaries and Wages Expense: $5,200
  • Supplies Expense: $1,500
  • Rent Expense: $900
  • Insurance Expense: $50
  • Interest Expense: $50
  • Depreciation Expense: $40
  • Dividends: $500

Closing Entry 1 - Close Revenues:

Oct. 31 | Service Revenue | 10,600 |
            | Income Summary | | 10,600
(To close revenue account)

Closing Entry 2 - Close Expenses:

Oct. 31 | Income Summary | 7,740 |
            | Salaries and Wages Expense | | 5,200
            | Supplies Expense | | 1,500
            | Rent Expense | | 900
            | Insurance Expense | | 50
            | Interest Expense | | 50
            | Depreciation Expense | | 40
(To close expense accounts)

Closing Entry 3 - Close Income Summary (Profit):

Oct. 31 | Income Summary | 2,860 |
            | Retained Earnings | | 2,860
(To close Income Summary - profit of $2,860)

Calculation: $10,600 revenues - $7,740 expenses = $2,860 profit

Closing Entry 4 - Close Dividends:

Oct. 31 | Retained Earnings | 500 |
            | Dividends | | 500
(To close Dividends account)

Result: After posting these entries:

  • All revenue accounts have zero balances
  • All expense accounts have zero balances
  • Income Summary has a zero balance
  • Dividends has a zero balance
  • Retained Earnings is updated: Beginning balance + Profit - Dividends = Ending balance

⚠️ Important Reminders

  • Closing entries are recorded in the general journal and posted to the ledger
  • The word "Closing Entry" is written in the description column of the journal
  • Closing entries are dated the last day of the accounting period
  • After closing, all temporary accounts have zero balances
  • Only permanent accounts (assets, liabilities, equity) have balances carried forward

Section 3: Post-Closing Trial Balance

What is a Post-Closing Trial Balance?

A post-closing trial balance is a list of all permanent accounts and their balances after closing entries have been journalized and posted.

Purpose:

  • To prove the equality of permanent account balances (debits = credits)
  • To verify that the closing process was completed correctly
  • To ensure all temporary accounts have been closed (zero balances)
  • To provide the opening balances for the next accounting period

Content of Post-Closing Trial Balance

Accounts Included:

  • All asset accounts (Cash, Accounts Receivable, Supplies, Equipment, etc.)
  • All liability accounts (Accounts Payable, Notes Payable, Salaries Payable, etc.)
  • All permanent equity accounts (Share Capital, Retained Earnings)

Accounts NOT Included:

  • Revenue accounts (closed to zero)
  • Expense accounts (closed to zero)
  • Dividends account (closed to zero)
  • Income Summary account (closed to zero)

Example: Post-Closing Trial Balance

Pioneer Advertising Inc. - Post-Closing Trial Balance, October 31

Account Debit Credit
Cash $15,200
Accounts Receivable $200
Supplies $1,000
Prepaid Insurance $550
Equipment $5,000
Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment $40
Notes Payable $5,000
Accounts Payable $2,500
Interest Payable $50
Salaries and Wages Payable $1,200
Unearned Service Revenue $800
Share Capital—Ordinary $10,000
Retained Earnings $2,360
Totals $21,950 $21,950

Note: The Retained Earnings balance of $2,360 reflects the updated balance after closing entries (beginning balance $0 + profit $2,860 - dividends $500 = $2,360).

⚠️ Key Points

  • The post-closing trial balance contains ONLY permanent accounts
  • If any temporary accounts appear, the closing process was not completed correctly
  • The balances in the post-closing trial balance become the opening balances for the next period
  • This is the final step before starting a new accounting period

Section 4: Summary of the Accounting Cycle

The Nine Steps of the Accounting Cycle

The accounting cycle is the series of steps performed during each accounting period to classify, record, and summarize financial data for a business. The cycle begins when a transaction occurs and ends with the preparation of a post-closing trial balance.

  1. Analyze Business Transactions

    Examine source documents (invoices, receipts, contracts) to determine the accounts affected and the amounts involved. Identify which accounts increase or decrease.

  2. Journalize the Transactions

    Record transactions in chronological order in the general journal. Include the date, accounts, amounts, and a brief explanation for each entry.

  3. Post to Ledger Accounts

    Transfer journal entries to the appropriate accounts in the general ledger. Update account balances after each posting.

  4. Prepare a Trial Balance

    List all ledger accounts and their balances at the end of the period. Verify that total debits equal total credits.

  5. Journalize and Post Adjusting Entries

    Record adjusting entries for accruals and deferrals at the end of the period. Post these entries to the ledger to update account balances.

  6. Prepare an Adjusted Trial Balance

    List all ledger accounts with their updated balances after adjusting entries. Verify that total debits equal total credits. This trial balance is used to prepare financial statements.

  7. Prepare Financial Statements

    Use the adjusted trial balance to prepare:

    • Income Statement (revenues and expenses)
    • Retained Earnings Statement (changes in retained earnings)
    • Statement of Financial Position (assets, liabilities, and equity)
    • Statement of Cash Flows (optional at this stage)
  8. Journalize and Post Closing Entries

    Record closing entries to transfer temporary account balances to Retained Earnings. Post these entries to the ledger. All revenue, expense, and dividend accounts should have zero balances after closing.

  9. Prepare a Post-Closing Trial Balance

    List all permanent accounts and their balances after closing entries. Verify that total debits equal total credits. This becomes the opening balance for the next accounting period.

Steps Performed Daily vs. Periodically

Steps Performed During the Period (Daily/Weekly):

  • Step 1: Analyze business transactions
  • Step 2: Journalize the transactions
  • Step 3: Post to ledger accounts

Steps Performed at the End of the Period (Monthly/Annually):

  • Step 4: Prepare a trial balance
  • Step 5: Journalize and post adjusting entries
  • Step 6: Prepare an adjusted trial balance
  • Step 7: Prepare financial statements
  • Step 8: Journalize and post closing entries
  • Step 9: Prepare a post-closing trial balance

⚠️ Optional Step: Reversing Entries

Some companies prepare reversing entries at the beginning of the next accounting period. These entries are the exact opposite of certain adjusting entries made at the end of the previous period. Reversing entries simplify the recording of subsequent transactions, particularly for accruals.

Section 5: Correcting Entries

What are Correcting Entries?

Correcting entries are journal entries made to correct errors in the accounting records. Errors can occur at any stage of the accounting process.

Important Distinction:

  • Adjusting entries are a planned part of the accounting cycle to update accounts at the end of a period
  • Correcting entries are made whenever an error is discovered, regardless of the timing

Types of Errors

1. Errors That Do NOT Affect Trial Balance Equality

These errors are harder to detect because debits still equal credits:

  • Error of omission: A transaction is completely omitted from the records
  • Error of commission: A transaction is recorded in the wrong account of the correct classification (e.g., debiting Office Supplies instead of Store Supplies)
  • Error of principle: A transaction violates an accounting principle (e.g., recording an asset purchase as an expense)
  • Compensating error: Two or more errors offset each other

2. Errors That DO Affect Trial Balance Equality

These errors are easier to detect because debits do not equal credits:

  • Recording only the debit or only the credit of a transaction
  • Recording different amounts for the debit and credit
  • Recording a debit as a credit, or vice versa
  • Making an error in addition or subtraction when calculating account balances
  • Transposition error (e.g., writing $63 instead of $36)
  • Slide error (e.g., writing $630 instead of $63)

How to Prepare Correcting Entries

Two-Step Approach:

  1. Determine what the correct entry should have been
  2. Compare the correct entry with the incorrect entry that was made

The correcting entry should bring the accounts from their incorrect balances to their correct balances.

Examples of Correcting Entries

Example 1: Error of Commission

Situation: On May 10, a $50 cash collection on account was journalized and posted as a debit to Cash $50 and a credit to Service Revenue $50 (instead of Accounts Receivable).

Incorrect Entry Made:

Dr. Cash | 50
    Cr. Service Revenue | 50

Correct Entry Should Have Been:

Dr. Cash | 50
    Cr. Accounts Receivable | 50

Correcting Entry:

Dr. Service Revenue | 50
    Cr. Accounts Receivable | 50
(To correct entry of May 10)

Explanation: Cash was correctly debited, so no correction is needed for Cash. Service Revenue was incorrectly credited and needs to be debited. Accounts Receivable was not credited but should have been, so it needs to be credited now.

Example 2: Error of Principle

Situation: On May 18, equipment costing $450 was purchased on account. The transaction was journalized and posted as a debit to Supplies Expense $450 and a credit to Accounts Payable $450.

Incorrect Entry Made:

Dr. Supplies Expense | 450
    Cr. Accounts Payable | 450

Correct Entry Should Have Been:

Dr. Equipment | 450
    Cr. Accounts Payable | 450

Correcting Entry:

Dr. Equipment | 450
    Cr. Supplies Expense | 450
(To correct entry of May 18)

Explanation: Accounts Payable was correctly credited, so no correction is needed. Supplies Expense was incorrectly debited and needs to be credited. Equipment was not debited but should have been, so it needs to be debited now.

Example 3: Incorrect Amount

Situation: On May 22, a $90 payment for salaries was journalized and posted as a debit to Salaries and Wages Expense $90 and a credit to Cash $9.

Incorrect Entry Made:

Dr. Salaries and Wages Expense | 90
    Cr. Cash | 9

Correct Entry Should Have Been:

Dr. Salaries and Wages Expense | 90
    Cr. Cash | 90

Correcting Entry:

Dr. Salaries and Wages Expense | 0
    Cr. Cash | 81
(To correct entry of May 22)

Explanation: Salaries and Wages Expense was correctly debited for $90. Cash was only credited for $9 but should have been credited for $90, so an additional credit of $81 is needed ($90 - $9 = $81).

⚠️ Important Points About Correcting Entries

  • Correcting entries should be made as soon as an error is discovered
  • Never erase or alter original journal entries or ledger postings
  • Always prepare a correcting entry and post it properly
  • Include a clear explanation in the correcting entry
  • If the error affects prior period financial statements, additional procedures may be required

Section 6: Classified Statement of Financial Position

What is a Classified Statement of Financial Position?

A classified statement of financial position (also called a classified balance sheet) groups together similar assets and similar liabilities using standard classifications and sections. This makes the statement more useful for analysis and decision-making.

Benefits:

  • Easier to identify key relationships and trends
  • Facilitates comparison between companies
  • Highlights liquidity (ability to pay short-term obligations)
  • Shows financial flexibility and solvency

Standard Classifications

A classified statement of financial position typically contains the following standard classifications:

ASSETS

  • Current Assets
  • Long-term Investments
  • Property, Plant, and Equipment
  • Intangible Assets

LIABILITIES

  • Current Liabilities
  • Non-current Liabilities (Long-term Liabilities)

EQUITY

  • Share Capital—Ordinary
  • Retained Earnings

Current Assets

Definition: Assets that a company expects to convert to cash or use up within one year or its operating cycle, whichever is longer.

Operating Cycle: The average time required to purchase inventory, sell it on account, and collect the cash from customers.

Common Current Assets (listed in order of liquidity):

  1. Cash: Currency, coins, and funds in bank accounts
  2. Short-term Investments: Investments that can be readily converted to cash (e.g., trading securities)
  3. Accounts Receivable: Amounts owed by customers
  4. Notes Receivable: Written promises to receive cash (due within one year)
  5. Inventory: Goods held for sale to customers
  6. Supplies: Items to be used in operations
  7. Prepaid Expenses: Expenses paid in advance (e.g., prepaid insurance, prepaid rent)

Long-term Investments

Definition: Resources that a company does not intend to convert to cash or use up within one year or the operating cycle.

Examples:

  • Investments in shares and bonds of other companies (held for long-term)
  • Investments in real estate not currently used in operations
  • Long-term notes receivable
  • Cash surrender value of life insurance

Property, Plant, and Equipment

Definition: Long-lived assets that a company uses in operations to generate revenue. Also called fixed assets or plant assets.

Characteristics:

  • Tangible (physical) assets
  • Used in operations (not held for resale)
  • Long-term in nature (useful life exceeds one year)
  • Subject to depreciation (except land)

Examples:

  • Land: Property owned (not depreciated)
  • Buildings: Structures used in operations
  • Equipment: Machinery, tools, vehicles, furniture
  • Delivery Equipment: Trucks, vans used for deliveries

Presentation: Property, plant, and equipment is shown at cost less accumulated depreciation. The difference is called book value or carrying amount.

Intangible Assets

Definition: Long-lived assets that lack physical substance but have value based on rights or privileges they confer on their owner.

Characteristics:

  • No physical form
  • Provide economic benefits over multiple periods
  • Subject to amortization (similar to depreciation)

Examples:

  • Patents: Exclusive rights to manufacture, sell, or control an invention (typically 20 years)
  • Copyrights: Exclusive rights to reproduce and sell artistic or published works
  • Trademarks: Words, phrases, or symbols that identify a particular enterprise or product
  • Trade Names: Brand names under which a product is sold
  • Franchises: Contractual arrangements to sell products or services in a specific area
  • Goodwill: The value of favorable attributes related to a business (only recorded when a business is purchased)

Current Liabilities

Definition: Obligations that a company expects to pay within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer.

Common Current Liabilities:

  • Accounts Payable: Amounts owed to suppliers for goods or services purchased on credit
  • Notes Payable: Written promises to pay (due within one year)
  • Salaries and Wages Payable: Amounts owed to employees
  • Interest Payable: Interest owed on borrowed funds
  • Taxes Payable: Income taxes or other taxes owed to government
  • Unearned Revenues: Cash received in advance for services or goods not yet provided
  • Current Portion of Long-term Debt: The portion of long-term debt due within one year

Non-current Liabilities

Definition: Obligations that a company does not expect to pay within one year or the operating cycle. Also called long-term liabilities.

Examples:

  • Bonds Payable: Long-term debt securities issued to investors
  • Long-term Notes Payable: Written promises to pay (due after one year)
  • Mortgage Payable: Debt secured by real estate
  • Lease Liabilities: Obligations under long-term lease agreements
  • Pension Liabilities: Obligations to provide retirement benefits to employees

Equity

Definition: The residual interest in the assets of a company after deducting liabilities. Also called shareholders' equity or stockholders' equity.

Components for a Corporation:

  • Share Capital—Ordinary: The amount paid by shareholders for shares of ownership in the company
  • Retained Earnings: Cumulative profit that has been retained in the business (not distributed as dividends)

Formula:

Equity = Assets - Liabilities

Note: For sole proprietorships, equity is called Owner's Capital. For partnerships, it is called Partners' Capital.

Example: Classified Statement of Financial Position

Pioneer Advertising Inc. - Statement of Financial Position, October 31

ASSETS
Current Assets
  Cash $15,200
  Accounts Receivable $200
  Supplies $1,000
  Prepaid Insurance $550
    Total Current Assets $16,950
Property, Plant, and Equipment
  Equipment $5,000
  Less: Accumulated Depreciation ($40)
    Total Property, Plant, and Equipment $4,960
TOTAL ASSETS $21,910
LIABILITIES AND EQUITY
Current Liabilities
  Accounts Payable $2,500
  Salaries and Wages Payable $1,200
  Interest Payable $50
  Unearned Service Revenue $800
    Total Current Liabilities $4,550
Non-current Liabilities
  Notes Payable $5,000
    Total Non-current Liabilities $5,000
Total Liabilities $9,550

  Retained Earnings $2,360

TOTAL LIABILITIES AND EQUITY $21,910

⚠️ Key Ratios from Classified Statement

Working Capital: Current Assets - Current Liabilities

Example: $16,950 - $4,550 = $12,400

Measures the company's ability to pay short-term obligations

Current Ratio: Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities

Example: $16,950 ÷ $4,550 = 3.73:1

Indicates liquidity; a ratio above 2:1 is generally considered healthy

Debt to Total Assets Ratio: Total Liabilities ÷ Total Assets

Example: $9,550 ÷ $21,910 = 0.44 or 44%

Shows the percentage of assets financed by debt; lower is generally better

Correct! A worksheet is an optional tool used internally to organize data and ensure accuracy. It is NOT a permanent record or financial statement.

  • Close Income Summary to Retained Earnings
  • Close revenue accounts to Income Summary

Correct! The first closing entry transfers all revenue account balances to Income Summary by debiting revenues and crediting Income Summary.

Correct! Income Summary is a temporary account used ONLY during closing to collect revenues and expenses before transferring the net amount to Retained Earnings.

  • Because dividends are a distribution of profit, not an expense
  • Because dividends are a permanent account that carries forward
  • Because dividends must be closed before revenues and expenses
Correct! Dividends represent a distribution of profit to shareholders, not a business expense. They are closed directly to Retained Earnings.

Question 8: Which accounts appear on a post-closing trial balance?

  • Only revenue and expense accounts after closing entries
  • Only permanent accounts: assets, liabilities, and equity

Correct! The post-closing trial balance contains only permanent accounts (assets, liabilities, equity) because all temporary accounts have been closed to zero.

Question 9: How many steps are in the complete accounting cycle?

  • Five steps, from analyzing transactions to preparing financial statements
  • Seven steps, ending with the adjusted trial balance
  • Nine steps, from analyzing transactions to post-closing trial balance

Correct! The accounting cycle consists of nine steps, beginning with analyzing transactions and ending with preparing a post-closing trial balance.

Question 10: Which step in the accounting cycle is performed daily or continuously?

  • Preparing the adjusted trial balance
  • Journalizing transactions
  • Preparing closing entries
  • Preparing the post-closing trial balance
Correct! Journalizing transactions (along with analyzing and posting) occurs daily as transactions happen, while the other steps occur at period-end.

Question 11: A correcting entry is needed when:

  • An error has been made in recording a transaction
  • Revenue needs to be recognized under accrual accounting
  • Temporary accounts need to be closed to permanent accounts
Correct! Correcting entries fix errors in the accounting records, while adjusting entries are routine period-end updates.

  • Original cost without any adjustments for depreciation
  • Replacement cost based on current market prices
Correct! Property, plant, and equipment is shown at cost minus accumulated depreciation, which equals book value or carrying amount.

Question 17: Which of the following is a current liability?

  • Bonds Payable due in 5 years
  • Unearned Service Revenue
  • Mortgage Payable due in 10 years
  • Long-term Notes Payable
Correct! Unearned Service Revenue is a current liability because the company expects to provide the service within one year. The others are non-current liabilities.

Question 18: Working capital is calculated as:

  • Total Assets minus Total Liabilities
  • Current Assets minus Current Liabilities
  • Total Assets minus Current Liabilities
  • Current Assets divided by Current Liabilities
Correct! Working capital = Current Assets - Current Liabilities. It measures the company's ability to pay short-term obligations.

Question 19: A current ratio of 2.5:1 means:

  • The company has $2.50 in total assets for every $1 in total liabilities
  • The company has $2.50 in current assets for every $1 in current liabilities
  • The company's profit margin is 2.5% of sales revenue
  • The company can pay its long-term debt 2.5 times over
Correct! The current ratio compares current assets to current liabilities. A ratio of 2.5:1 indicates strong liquidity.

Question 20: The operating cycle is:

  • Always exactly one year for all companies
  • The average time to purchase inventory, sell it, and collect cash
  • The time between preparing financial statements
  • The period from hiring employees to paying their salaries
Correct! The operating cycle is the time from purchasing inventory to collecting cash from customers. It varies by industry.

Question 21: Goodwill is recorded on the books only when:

  • A company develops a strong reputation over many years
  • A company purchases another business
  • A company's stock price increases significantly
  • A company receives positive customer reviews
Correct! Goodwill is only recorded when one company purchases another business and pays more than the fair value of identifiable net assets.

Question 22: After all closing entries are posted, which account will NOT have a zero balance?

  • Service Revenue
  • Salaries and Wages Expense
  • Accumulated Depreciation
  • Dividends
Correct! Accumulated Depreciation is a permanent account (contra-asset) and is NOT closed. The others are temporary accounts that are closed to zero.

Question 23: If total debits in the adjusted trial balance equal $85,000 and total credits equal $85,000, but revenues total $40,000 and expenses total $28,000, what is the profit?

  • $85,000
  • $28,000
  • $12,000
  • $40,000
Correct! Profit = Revenues - Expenses = $40,000 - $28,000 = $12,000. The trial balance totals are not used to calculate profit.

Question 24: Which financial statement is prepared FIRST from the worksheet?

  • Statement of Financial Position
  • Retained Earnings Statement
  • Income Statement
  • Statement of Cash Flows
Correct! The Income Statement is prepared first because its profit/loss figure is needed for the Retained Earnings Statement, which in turn is needed for the Statement of Financial Position.

Question 25: The debt to total assets ratio measures:

  • The company's ability to pay current liabilities with current assets
  • The percentage of total assets financed by creditors
  • The company's profitability relative to its asset base
  • The efficiency of asset utilization in generating revenue
Correct! Debt to Total Assets Ratio = Total Liabilities ÷ Total Assets. It shows what percentage of assets are financed by debt rather than equity.

Case Study 2: Closing Entries

Scenario: Tech Solutions Inc. has the following account balances after adjustments on June 30:

  • Consulting Revenue: $45,000
  • Salaries and Wages Expense: $60,000
  • Rent Expense: $18,000
  • Utilities Expense: $8,000
  • Depreciation Expense: $12,000
  • Supplies Expense: $5,000
  • Dividends: $10,000
  • Retained Earnings (beginning): $25,000

Question: Prepare all four closing entries and calculate the ending Retained Earnings balance.

Solution:

Closing Entry 1 - Close Revenues:

Dr. Service Revenue 95,000
Dr. Consulting Revenue 45,000
    Cr. Income Summary 140,000

Case Study 3: Correcting Entries

Scenario: During an audit of Green Landscaping, the following errors were discovered:

  1. A $1,200 payment for equipment was recorded as: Dr. Repairs Expense 1,200 / Cr. Cash 1,200
  2. A $500 collection on account was recorded as: Dr. Cash 500 / Cr. Service Revenue 500
  3. A $300 purchase of supplies on account was completely omitted from the records

Question: Prepare the correcting entries for each error.

Solution:

Correcting Entry 1 (Error of Principle):

Dr. Equipment 1,200
    Cr. Repairs Expense 1,200
(To correct recording of equipment purchase as expense)

Correcting Entry 2 (Error of Commission):

Dr. Service Revenue 500
    Cr. Accounts Receivable 500
(To correct recording of collection on account)

Correcting Entry 3 (Error of Omission):

Dr. Supplies 300
    Cr. Accounts Payable 300
(To record omitted purchase of supplies on account)

Explanation: Error 1 is an error of principle (asset recorded as expense). Error 2 is an error of commission (wrong revenue account). Error 3 is an error of omission (transaction not recorded).

Case Study 4: Classified Statement of Financial Position

  • Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment: $20,000
  • Patent: $15,000

  • Salaries Payable: $3,500
  • Notes Payable (due in 3 years): $40,000
  • Share Capital: $50,000
  • Retained Earnings: $36,800

Question: Calculate (a) Total Current Assets, (b) Total Assets, (c) Working Capital, and (d) Current Ratio.

Solution:

Cash $12,000 + Accounts Receivable $18,000 + Inventory $35,000 + Supplies $2,500 + Prepaid Insurance $1,800 = $69,300

  • Current Assets: $69,300
  • Property, Plant & Equipment: $80,000 - $20,000 = $60,000
  • Intangible Assets: $15,000

Current Assets $69,300 - Current Liabilities ($14,000 + $3,500) = $69,300 - $17,500 = $51,800
