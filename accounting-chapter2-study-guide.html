📚 The Recording Process

Chapter 2 - Financial Accounting IFRS 3rd Edition

What is an Account?

Definition

An account is an individual record of increases and decreases in a specific asset, liability, equity, revenue, or expense item.

T-Account Form

Accounts can be illustrated in a T-account form, which shows the basic structure:

Account Name
Debit / Dr.
(Left Side)
Credit / Cr.
(Right Side)

Key Terminology

  • Debit (Dr.): The left side of an account
  • Credit (Cr.): The right side of an account
  • Balance: The difference between total debits and total credits
Important Note: "Debit" and "Credit" are simply accounting terms meaning "left" and "right." They do NOT mean "increase" or "decrease" by themselves - the meaning depends on the type of account.

Debits and Credits

The Double-Entry System

The double-entry system is the foundation of accounting. Key principles:

  • Each transaction must affect two or more accounts
  • Recording is done by debiting at least one account and crediting at least one other account
  • DEBITS must equal CREDITS for every transaction
  • This keeps the accounting equation in balance

Memory Aid: DEALER

The first three (DEA) increase with debits, the last three (LER) increase with credits.

Practical Example: Analyzing Transactions

Transaction 1: Owner invests €10,000 cash in the business

Analysis:

  • Cash (Asset) increases by €10,000 → Debit Cash
  • Share Capital (Equity) increases by €10,000 → Credit Share Capital

Journal Entry:

Cash €10,000
Share Capital €10,000

Transaction 2: Purchased equipment for €5,000 cash

Analysis:

  • Equipment (Asset) increases by €5,000 → Debit Equipment
  • Cash (Asset) decreases by €5,000 → Credit Cash

Journal Entry:

Equipment €5,000
Cash €5,000

Steps in the Recording Process

The recording process involves three basic steps:

Step 1: Analyze Each Transaction

Determine what accounts are affected and classify them by type:

  • Identify the accounts involved
  • Classify each account (Asset, Liability, Equity, Revenue, or Expense)
  • Determine whether each account increases or decreases

Example:

Transaction: Paid €1,200 for rent

Analysis:

  • Rent Expense (Expense account) increases
  • Cash (Asset account) decreases

Step 2: Enter Transaction in a Journal

Record the transaction in the journal using debits and credits:

  • Apply debit and credit rules based on account types
  • Ensure debits equal credits
  • Include a brief explanation
  • Record transactions in chronological order

Example (continuing from above):

Date Account Titles and Explanation Debit Credit
Oct. 1 Rent Expense
Cash
(Paid October rent)		 €1,200
€1,200

Step 3: Transfer Journal Information to Ledger Accounts

Post the journal entries to the general ledger:

  • Transfer debit and credit amounts to the appropriate ledger accounts
  • Update account balances
  • This process is called "posting"
Key Point: These three steps ensure that all transactions are systematically recorded and organized, maintaining the integrity of the accounting records.

The Journal

What is a Journal?

A journal (also called the "book of original entry") is an accounting record where transactions are initially recorded in chronological order.

Functions of the Journal

  • Discloses the complete effect of a transaction in one place
  • Provides a chronological record of all transactions
  • Helps prevent or locate errors because debits and credits can be easily compared

The Journalizing Process

Journalizing is the process of entering transaction data in the journal. Follow these steps:

Steps to Journalize a Transaction:

  1. Date: Enter the date of the transaction in the Date column
  2. Debit Entry: Enter the account title to be debited at the left margin, followed by the amount in the Debit column
  3. Credit Entry: Enter the account title to be credited, indented, followed by the amount in the Credit column
  4. Explanation: Write a brief explanation of the transaction below the credit entry
  5. Space: Leave a blank line between journal entries

Types of Journal Entries

1. Simple Entry

A simple entry involves only two accounts (one debit and one credit).

Example: Received €5,000 cash from customers for services performed

Date Account Titles and Explanation Debit Credit
Oct. 5 Cash
Service Revenue
(Received cash for services)		 €5,000
€5,000

2. Compound Entry

A compound entry involves three or more accounts (multiple debits and/or credits).

Example: Purchased equipment for €8,000, paying €3,000 cash and signing a note payable for €5,000

Date Account Titles and Explanation Debit Credit
Oct. 10 Equipment
Cash
Notes Payable
(Purchased equipment with cash and note)		 €8,000
€3,000
€5,000

Analysis: Equipment increases (debit €8,000), Cash decreases (credit €3,000), Notes Payable increases (credit €5,000). Total debits (€8,000) = Total credits (€8,000).

Important Rules:
  • The debit account(s) are always listed first
  • Credit account(s) are indented
  • Total debits must always equal total credits
  • Explanations should be brief but clear

The Ledger

What is a Ledger?

The general ledger (or simply "ledger") contains all the asset, liability, equity, revenue, and expense accounts. It is the entire group of accounts maintained by a company.

Chart of Accounts

A chart of accounts is a listing of all accounts and their account numbers in the ledger. Accounts are usually numbered for easy identification and reference.

Typical Numbering System:

Account Type Number Range Examples
Assets 100-199 101 Cash, 112 Accounts Receivable, 157 Equipment
Liabilities 200-299 201 Accounts Payable, 230 Notes Payable
Equity 300-399 311 Share Capital, 320 Retained Earnings, 332 Dividends
Revenues 400-499 400 Service Revenue, 410 Sales Revenue
Expenses 500-599 500 Salaries Expense, 510 Rent Expense, 520 Utilities Expense

Standard Form of Account

The standard form of account has three money columns:

  • Debit column: Records debit entries
  • Credit column: Records credit entries
  • Balance column: Shows the account balance after each transaction

Example: Cash Account in Standard Form

Cash (Account No. 101)
Date Explanation Ref. Debit Credit Balance
Oct. 1 Investment J1 €10,000 €10,000
Oct. 3 Equipment purchase J1 €5,000 €5,000
Oct. 5 Service revenue J1 €3,000 €8,000
Key Features:
  • The Ref. column indicates the journal page from which the entry was posted
  • The Balance column is updated after each transaction
  • This format makes it easy to determine the current balance at any time

Posting

What is Posting?

Posting is the process of transferring journal entries to the ledger accounts. This is the third step in the recording process.

The Posting Process

Follow these steps when posting from the journal to the ledger:

Steps to Post a Journal Entry:

  1. In the ledger: Enter the date from the journal in the Date column of the ledger account
  2. In the ledger: Enter the amount from the journal in the appropriate Debit or Credit column
  3. In the ledger: Enter the journal page number in the Reference column
  4. In the ledger: Calculate and enter the new balance in the Balance column
  5. In the journal: Enter the ledger account number in the Reference column (this indicates the entry has been posted)

Illustration of Posting

Journal Entry:

Date Account Titles and Explanation Ref. Debit Credit
Oct. 1 Cash
Share Capital
(Owner investment)		 101
311		 €10,000
€10,000

After Posting to Ledger:

Cash Account (No. 101):

Important Notes:
  • Posting should be performed in chronological order
  • All journal entries must be posted to maintain accurate records
  • The reference numbers create a cross-reference between journal and ledger
  • This cross-referencing is called the audit trail

The Trial Balance

What is a Trial Balance?

A trial balance is a list of all accounts and their balances at a specific time. It is usually prepared at the end of an accounting period.

Purpose of the Trial Balance

  • Prove the mathematical equality of debits and credits after posting
  • Uncover errors in journalizing and posting
  • Provide a basis for preparing financial statements

Preparing a Trial Balance

Steps to Prepare a Trial Balance:

  1. List account titles in the order they appear in the ledger (Assets, Liabilities, Equity, Revenues, Expenses)
  2. Enter account balances in the appropriate Debit or Credit column
  3. Total the Debit and Credit columns
  4. Verify equality - Total debits must equal total credits

Example: Trial Balance

ABC Company
Trial Balance
October 31, 2024
Account Debit Credit
Cash €15,200
Accounts Receivable €7,000
Supplies €2,500
Equipment €25,000
Accounts Payable €4,500
Notes Payable €10,000
Share Capital €20,000
Dividends €1,000
Service Revenue €24,000
Salaries Expense €5,200
Rent Expense €1,800
Utilities Expense €800
Totals €58,500 €58,500

Limitations of a Trial Balance

A trial balance does NOT prove that all transactions have been recorded or that the ledger is correct. It only proves that debits equal credits.

Errors NOT Detected by a Trial Balance:

  • Transaction not journalized: If a transaction is completely omitted, the trial balance will still balance
  • Correct journal entry not posted: If an entry is journalized but never posted, debits still equal credits
  • Journal entry posted twice: Posting the same entry twice keeps debits equal to credits
  • Wrong accounts used: If you debit/credit the wrong accounts but use correct amounts, the trial balance balances
  • Offsetting errors: Two errors that cancel each other out (e.g., €100 overstatement and €100 understatement)

Currency Signs and Underlining

Conventions for Trial Balance Presentation:

  • Currency signs (€, $, £, etc.) appear only at the top of each column and for totals
  • A single line is placed under the last figure in each column before totaling
  • Double lines (double underline) are placed under the final totals
  • These conventions apply to all financial statements
Key Takeaway: The trial balance is a useful tool for checking mathematical accuracy, but it does not guarantee that all transactions are correctly recorded. Additional review and analysis are always necessary.

CASE STUDY 1: On January 5, Martinez Company purchased office supplies for €2,500 on account from Office Depot. What is the correct journal entry?
a) Debit Supplies €2,500; Credit Cash €2,500
b) Debit Accounts Payable €2,500; Credit Supplies €2,500
c) Debit Supplies €2,500; Credit Accounts Payable €2,500
d) Debit Cash €2,500; Credit Supplies €2,500
Correct Answer: c) Debit Supplies €2,500; Credit Accounts Payable €2,500
Explanation: When purchasing supplies on account, Supplies (an asset) increases with a debit, and Accounts Payable (a liability) increases with a credit. No cash is involved since it's purchased "on account."
CASE STUDY 2: Thompson Services received €8,000 cash from clients for services performed. After this transaction, what happens to the accounting equation?
a) Assets increase €8,000; Liabilities increase €8,000
b) Assets increase €8,000; Equity increases €8,000
c) Assets decrease €8,000; Equity decreases €8,000
d) Assets increase €8,000; Expenses increase €8,000
Correct Answer: b) Assets increase €8,000; Equity increases €8,000
Explanation: Cash (asset) increases by €8,000, and Service Revenue (which increases equity) also increases by €8,000. Revenue increases retained earnings, which is part of equity. The accounting equation remains balanced: Assets = Liabilities + Equity.
CASE STUDY 3: Wilson Corporation paid €3,600 for a one-year insurance policy on March 1. The policy covers March 1 through February 28 of the next year. What accounts are affected?
a) Debit Insurance Expense €3,600; Credit Cash €3,600
b) Debit Prepaid Insurance €3,600; Credit Cash €3,600
c) Debit Cash €3,600; Credit Insurance Expense €3,600
d) Debit Accounts Payable €3,600; Credit Cash €3,600
Correct Answer: b) Debit Prepaid Insurance €3,600; Credit Cash €3,600
Explanation: Since the insurance covers a future period (one year), it is recorded as Prepaid Insurance (an asset), not as an expense. Cash decreases (credit). The insurance will be expensed gradually over the coverage period through adjusting entries.
CASE STUDY 4: Anderson Company purchased equipment for €15,000, paying €5,000 cash and signing a note payable for the balance. How many accounts are affected and what is the compound entry?
a) Two accounts: Debit Equipment €15,000; Credit Cash €15,000
b) Three accounts: Debit Equipment €15,000; Credit Cash €5,000 and Notes Payable €10,000
c) Two accounts: Debit Equipment €5,000; Credit Cash €5,000
d) Three accounts: Debit Cash €5,000 and Notes Payable €10,000; Credit Equipment €15,000
Correct Answer: b) Three accounts: Debit Equipment €15,000; Credit Cash €5,000 and Notes Payable €10,000
Explanation: This is a compound entry involving three accounts. Equipment (asset) increases by the full purchase price of €15,000 (debit). Cash (asset) decreases by €5,000 (credit), and Notes Payable (liability) increases by €10,000 (credit). Total debits (€15,000) equal total credits (€5,000 + €10,000 = €15,000).
CASE STUDY 5: Roberts Company's trial balance shows total debits of €125,000 and total credits of €128,000. What does this indicate?
a) The company has a net income of €3,000
b) There is an error in the accounting records
c) The company owes €3,000 to creditors
d) The trial balance is correct and balanced
Correct Answer: b) There is an error in the accounting records
Explanation: A trial balance must have equal debits and credits. If total debits (€125,000) do not equal total credits (€128,000), there is a €3,000 difference indicating an error in journalizing, posting, or calculating account balances. The error must be found and corrected before proceeding with financial statements.
