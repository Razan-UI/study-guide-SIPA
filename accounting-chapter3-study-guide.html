1. Timing Issues in Accounting

1.1 The Time Period Assumption

Definition: The time period assumption (or periodicity assumption) states that the economic life of a business can be divided into artificial time periods for reporting purposes.

Key Concepts:

  • Purpose: Allows businesses to report financial information at regular intervals
  • Common Periods: Monthly, quarterly, semi-annually, and annually
  • Importance: Enables timely decision-making by stakeholders
  • Trade-off: Timeliness vs. Accuracy (shorter periods may be less accurate but more timely)

1.2 Fiscal Year vs. Calendar Year

Calendar Year:

  • Begins January 1 and ends December 31
  • Most commonly used by individuals and many businesses
  • Aligns with tax reporting for many jurisdictions

Fiscal Year:

  • Any 12-month accounting period used by a business
  • Can begin on any date and end 12 months later
  • Often chosen to align with business cycles
  • Example: A retail company might use February 1 - January 31 to avoid closing during the busy holiday season

Example: Choosing a Fiscal Year

Scenario: A ski resort business experiences peak operations from November through March.

Calendar Year Problem: If using a calendar year (Jan 1 - Dec 31), the busy season would be split across two reporting periods, making performance analysis difficult.

Fiscal Year Solution: The company chooses a fiscal year of April 1 - March 31, capturing the entire ski season in one reporting period.

Benefit: Better matching of revenues and expenses, clearer performance evaluation.

1.3 Interim Periods

Definition: Interim periods are accounting time periods shorter than a full year.

Types of Interim Periods:

  • Monthly: 1-month periods (12 per year)
  • Quarterly: 3-month periods (4 per year) - most common for public companies
  • Semi-annual: 6-month periods (2 per year)

Purpose:

  • Provide timely information to users
  • Allow for early detection of problems or opportunities
  • Meet regulatory requirements (e.g., quarterly reports for public companies)
  • Support management decision-making

2. Accrual-Basis vs. Cash-Basis Accounting

2.1 Cash-Basis Accounting

Definition: Under cash-basis accounting, revenues are recorded when cash is received, and expenses are recorded when cash is paid.

Characteristics:

  • Simple and easy to understand
  • Based on actual cash flows
  • Does not require complex adjustments
  • Commonly used by small businesses and individuals

Limitations:

  • Does not match revenues with related expenses
  • Can be manipulated by timing cash receipts and payments
  • Does not provide accurate picture of financial position
  • Not acceptable under IFRS or GAAP for most businesses
  • Ignores receivables and payables

2.2 Accrual-Basis Accounting

Definition: Under accrual-basis accounting, transactions are recorded in the periods in which the events occur, regardless of when cash is received or paid.

Characteristics:

  • Required by IFRS and GAAP
  • Provides better matching of revenues and expenses
  • More accurately reflects financial position and performance
  • Requires adjusting entries at period-end
  • More complex than cash-basis accounting

Advantages:

  • Better reflects economic reality
  • Matches revenues with expenses that generated them
  • Provides more useful information for decision-making
  • Recognizes all assets and liabilities
  • Cannot be easily manipulated by timing cash flows

Example: Cash-Basis vs. Accrual-Basis

Scenario: On December 15, 2024, ABC Company performs services for a client worth $5,000. The client pays on January 10, 2025.

Accounting Method When Revenue is Recorded 2024 Revenue 2025 Revenue
Cash-Basis January 10, 2025 (when cash received) $0 $5,000
Accrual-Basis December 15, 2024 (when service performed) $5,000 $0

Analysis: Accrual-basis accounting properly matches the revenue with the period in which the service was performed, providing a more accurate picture of 2024 operations.

⚠️ Important Note

While cash-basis accounting is simpler, it is generally not acceptable for financial reporting under IFRS or GAAP. Most businesses must use accrual-basis accounting to comply with accounting standards and provide meaningful financial information to users.

3. Revenue and Expense Recognition Principles

3.1 Revenue Recognition Principle

Definition: The revenue recognition principle requires that companies recognize revenue in the accounting period in which the performance obligation is satisfied.

Key Criteria for Revenue Recognition (IFRS 15):

  1. Identify the contract with the customer: A contract exists when there is an agreement between parties that creates enforceable rights and obligations.
  2. Identify the performance obligations: Determine what goods or services the company has promised to transfer to the customer.
  3. Determine the transaction price: Identify the amount of consideration the company expects to receive in exchange for transferring goods or services.
  4. Allocate the transaction price: If multiple performance obligations exist, allocate the transaction price to each obligation.
  5. Recognize revenue when performance obligation is satisfied: Revenue is recognized when control of the goods or services is transferred to the customer.

When is Revenue Recognized?

  • For goods: When control transfers to the buyer (usually at delivery)
  • For services: When the service is performed
  • Over time: For long-term contracts, revenue may be recognized as work progresses

Example: Revenue Recognition

Scenario 1 - Service Revenue:

On October 20, a law firm completes legal work for a client and bills $3,000. The client pays on November 15.

Revenue Recognition Date: October 20 (when service is performed)

Journal Entry on October 20:

Accounts Receivable ............ 3,000
    Service Revenue ........................... 3,000

Scenario 2 - Product Sales:

On December 28, a retailer delivers goods to a customer worth $2,500. The customer pays on January 5.

Revenue Recognition Date: December 28 (when goods are delivered)

Journal Entry on December 28:

Accounts Receivable ............ 2,500
    Sales Revenue ............................... 2,500

3.2 Expense Recognition Principle (Matching Principle)

Definition: The expense recognition principle (also called the matching principle) requires that expenses be recognized in the same period as the revenues they helped to generate.

Three Methods of Expense Recognition:

  1. Direct Association (Cause and Effect): Match expenses directly with revenues when there is a clear relationship (e.g., cost of goods sold matched with sales revenue).
  2. Systematic and Rational Allocation: Allocate expenses over multiple periods when they benefit multiple periods (e.g., depreciation of equipment over its useful life).
  3. Immediate Recognition: Recognize expenses immediately when there is no clear future benefit or when the cost cannot be reliably measured (e.g., advertising expenses, administrative salaries).

Example: Expense Recognition Methods

Method 1 - Direct Association:

A company sells inventory that cost $1,000 for $1,500 in March.

Recognition: The $1,000 cost of goods sold is recognized in March, the same period as the $1,500 sales revenue.

Method 2 - Systematic Allocation:

A company purchases equipment for $12,000 with a 5-year useful life.

Recognition: Depreciation expense of $2,400 per year ($12,000 ÷ 5 years) is recognized each year over the equipment's useful life.

Method 3 - Immediate Recognition:

A company pays $5,000 for advertising in June.

Recognition: The entire $5,000 is recognized as expense in June because the future benefit cannot be reliably measured.

⚠️ Key Principle

Let expenses follow revenues. The goal is to match expenses with the revenues they help generate, not necessarily with cash payments. This provides a more accurate measure of profitability for each period.

4. The Need for Adjusting Entries

4.1 What are Adjusting Entries?

Definition: Adjusting entries are journal entries made at the end of an accounting period to record revenues and expenses in the period in which they occur, ensuring that the revenue recognition and expense recognition principles are followed.

Why are Adjusting Entries Necessary?

  • Some events are not recorded daily because it is not efficient to do so
  • Some costs expire with the passage of time rather than through transactions
  • Some items may be unrecorded at the end of the period
  • To ensure revenues and expenses are recorded in the proper period
  • To update asset and liability accounts to their proper balances

Characteristics of Adjusting Entries:

  • Made at the end of the accounting period
  • Always involve at least one income statement account (revenue or expense)
  • Always involve at least one balance sheet account (asset or liability)
  • Never involve cash
  • Required for accrual-basis accounting

4.2 Types of Adjusting Entries

There are four main types of adjusting entries, which can be categorized into two groups:

Category Type Description Accounts Affected
DEFERRALS
(Prepayments)		 1. Prepaid Expenses Expenses paid in cash before they are used or consumed Asset → Expense
2. Unearned Revenues Cash received before services are performed or goods delivered Liability → Revenue
ACCRUALS 3. Accrued Revenues Revenues for services performed but not yet received in cash or recorded Asset ↑ / Revenue ↑
4. Accrued Expenses Expenses incurred but not yet paid in cash or recorded Expense ↑ / Liability ↑

⚠️ Critical Rule

Adjusting entries NEVER involve cash. If cash is involved, the entry is not an adjusting entry—it's a regular transaction entry. Adjusting entries are made to recognize revenues and expenses that have occurred but have not yet been recorded through normal cash transactions.

Visual Summary: Deferrals vs. Accruals

DEFERRALS (Cash First, Recognition Later):

Prepaid Expenses: Pay Cash → Record Asset → Adjust to Expense (as used)

Unearned Revenues: Receive Cash → Record Liability → Adjust to Revenue (as earned)

ACCRUALS (Recognition First, Cash Later):

Accrued Revenues: Perform Service → Adjust to Record Revenue & Receivable → Receive Cash

Accrued Expenses: Incur Expense → Adjust to Record Expense & Payable → Pay Cash

5. Adjusting Entries for Deferrals

5.1 Prepaid Expenses (Deferred Expenses)

Definition: Prepaid expenses are costs that have been paid in cash and recorded as assets before they are used or consumed. As time passes or the asset is used, the prepaid expense becomes an actual expense.

Common Examples:

  • Prepaid Insurance
  • Prepaid Rent
  • Office Supplies
  • Prepaid Advertising
  • Equipment (through depreciation)

The Adjusting Entry Process:

  1. Initial Transaction: When payment is made, debit an asset account (e.g., Prepaid Insurance) and credit Cash.
  2. Period-End Analysis: Determine how much of the prepaid expense has been used or expired during the period.
  3. Adjusting Entry: Debit an expense account and credit the asset account for the amount used/expired.
Adjusting Entry Format:
Expense Account (e.g., Insurance Expense) .......... XXX
    Prepaid Asset Account (e.g., Prepaid Insurance) ............ XXX

Example 1: Prepaid Insurance

Scenario: On October 1, 2024, Pioneer Advertising Agency paid $6,000 for a 12-month insurance policy covering October 1, 2024 through September 30, 2025.

Step 1 - Initial Entry (October 1, 2024):

Prepaid Insurance .................. 6,000
    Cash .......................................... 6,000
(To record purchase of 12-month insurance policy)

Step 2 - Calculate Expired Portion (October 31, 2024):

  • Total policy cost: $6,000
  • Policy period: 12 months
  • Monthly cost: $6,000 ÷ 12 = $500 per month
  • Time expired: 1 month (October)
  • Amount expired: $500 × 1 = $500

Step 3 - Adjusting Entry (October 31, 2024):

Insurance Expense .................. 500
    Prepaid Insurance .................... 500
(To record insurance expense for October)

Result After Adjustment:

Account Balance Financial Statement
Prepaid Insurance (Asset) $5,500 ($6,000 - $500) Balance Sheet
Insurance Expense $500 Income Statement

Analysis: The adjusting entry properly allocates the insurance cost to the period that benefited from the coverage (October), while the remaining $5,500 stays as an asset because it represents future benefits (11 months of coverage remaining).

Example 2: Supplies

Scenario: On October 5, Pioneer Advertising purchased $2,500 of office supplies. On October 31, a physical count shows $1,000 of supplies remaining.

Step 1 - Initial Entry (October 5):

Supplies .................................. 2,500
    Cash .......................................... 2,500
(To record purchase of supplies)

Step 2 - Calculate Supplies Used (October 31):

  • Supplies purchased: $2,500
  • Supplies on hand (per count): $1,000
  • Supplies used: $2,500 - $1,000 = $1,500

Step 3 - Adjusting Entry (October 31):

Supplies Expense .................... 1,500
    Supplies ..................................... 1,500
(To record supplies used during October)

Result After Adjustment:

Account Balance Meaning
Supplies (Asset) $1,000 Supplies still available for future use
Supplies Expense $1,500 Cost of supplies consumed in October

5.2 Depreciation

Definition: Depreciation is the process of allocating the cost of a long-lived asset (such as equipment, buildings, or vehicles) to expense over its useful life in a rational and systematic manner.

Key Concepts:

  • Purpose: Match the cost of the asset with the revenues it helps generate over its useful life
  • Not a valuation process: Depreciation does not attempt to measure the decline in market value
  • Not a cash transaction: No cash changes hands when recording depreciation
  • Applies to: Property, plant, and equipment (PP&E) - NOT land

Factors in Computing Depreciation:

  1. Cost: The original purchase price plus all costs necessary to get the asset ready for use (delivery, installation, testing, etc.)
  2. Useful Life: An estimate of how long the asset will be productive (in years or units of activity)
  3. Residual Value (Salvage Value): The estimated value of the asset at the end of its useful life
Straight-Line Depreciation Formula:

Annual Depreciation Expense = (Cost - Residual Value) ÷ Useful Life in Years

OR

Depreciable Amount ÷ Useful Life = Annual Depreciation

Accumulated Depreciation:

  • Definition: A contra-asset account that shows the total depreciation taken on an asset since it was acquired
  • Normal Balance: Credit (opposite of asset accounts)
  • Purpose: Allows users to see both the original cost and total depreciation
  • Book Value: Cost - Accumulated Depreciation = Book Value (carrying value)

Example 3: Depreciation of Equipment

Scenario: On October 1, Pioneer Advertising purchased equipment for $50,000. The equipment has an estimated useful life of 5 years and a residual value of $2,000.

Step 1 - Calculate Annual Depreciation:

Cost: $50,000
Less: Residual Value: $2,000
Depreciable Amount: $48,000

Annual Depreciation = $48,000 ÷ 5 years = $9,600 per year
Monthly Depreciation = $9,600 ÷ 12 months = $800 per month

Step 2 - Adjusting Entry (October 31):

Depreciation Expense .............. 800
    Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment .... 800
(To record depreciation for October)

Balance Sheet Presentation (October 31):

Equipment .................................................... $50,000
Less: Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment ... (800)
Book Value .................................................. $49,200

After One Year (October 1, Year 2):

Equipment .................................................... $50,000
Less: Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment ... (9,600)
Book Value .................................................. $40,400

Important Notes:

  • The Equipment account always shows the original cost ($50,000)
  • Accumulated Depreciation increases each period
  • Book value decreases each period
  • After 5 years, book value will equal residual value ($2,000)

⚠️ Why Use Accumulated Depreciation?

Instead of crediting the Equipment account directly, we use Accumulated Depreciation because:

  • It preserves the original cost of the asset on the balance sheet
  • It shows the total depreciation taken over the asset's life
  • It allows calculation of book value (Cost - Accumulated Depreciation)
  • It provides useful information about the age and condition of assets

5.3 Unearned Revenues (Deferred Revenues)

Definition: Unearned revenues are cash receipts that have been received and recorded as liabilities before services are performed or goods are delivered. As the company performs the service or delivers the goods, the liability decreases and revenue is recognized.

Common Examples:

  • Advance ticket sales (airlines, concerts, sports events)
  • Magazine subscriptions received in advance
  • Rent received in advance from tenants
  • Customer deposits
  • Gift card sales
  • Prepaid service contracts

The Adjusting Entry Process:

  1. Initial Transaction: When cash is received in advance, debit Cash and credit a liability account (e.g., Unearned Service Revenue).
  2. Period-End Analysis: Determine how much of the service has been performed or goods delivered during the period.
  3. Adjusting Entry: Debit the liability account and credit a revenue account for the amount earned.
Adjusting Entry Format:
Unearned Revenue (Liability) .......... XXX
    Revenue Account ............................ XXX

Example 4: Unearned Service Revenue

Scenario: On October 2, Pioneer Advertising received $12,000 cash from a client for advertising services to be performed over the next 12 months (October 2024 through September 2025).

Step 1 - Initial Entry (October 2):

Cash .......................................... 12,000
    Unearned Service Revenue ........ 12,000
(To record receipt of advance payment)

Analysis: At this point, Pioneer has a liability because it owes services to the client. The company has received cash but has not yet earned the revenue.

Step 2 - Calculate Revenue Earned (October 31):

  • Total advance payment: $12,000
  • Service period: 12 months
  • Monthly revenue: $12,000 ÷ 12 = $1,000 per month
  • Time elapsed: 1 month (October)
  • Revenue earned: $1,000 × 1 = $1,000

Step 3 - Adjusting Entry (October 31):

Unearned Service Revenue .......... 1,000
    Service Revenue ........................ 1,000
(To record revenue earned in October)

Result After Adjustment:

Account Balance Financial Statement Meaning
Unearned Service Revenue (Liability) $11,000 Balance Sheet Services still owed to client (11 months remaining)
Service Revenue $1,000 Income Statement Revenue earned in October

Each Month: Pioneer will make the same adjusting entry, reducing the liability by $1,000 and recognizing $1,000 of revenue, until the entire $12,000 has been earned by September 2025.

Example 5: Magazine Subscriptions

Scenario: On July 1, a magazine publisher receives $3,600 for 100 one-year subscriptions ($36 each). The magazines are published monthly.

Initial Entry (July 1):

Cash .......................................... 3,600
    Unearned Subscription Revenue .. 3,600

Monthly Calculation:

  • Total subscriptions: $3,600
  • Subscription period: 12 months
  • Monthly revenue: $3,600 ÷ 12 = $300 per month

Adjusting Entry (July 31):

Unearned Subscription Revenue .... 300
    Subscription Revenue ................. 300
(To record revenue earned for July issue)

After 6 Months (December 31):

  • Total revenue recognized: $300 × 6 = $1,800
  • Remaining liability: $3,600 - $1,800 = $1,800

⚠️ Key Distinction: Asset vs. Liability

Prepaid Expenses (Asset): We paid cash and have the right to receive future benefits → Asset

Unearned Revenues (Liability): We received cash and have the obligation to provide future services → Liability

Both involve cash received or paid before recognition, but from opposite perspectives!

6. Adjusting Entries for Accruals

6.1 Accrued Revenues (Accrued Assets)

Definition: Accrued revenues are revenues that have been earned but not yet received in cash or recorded. These represent services performed or goods delivered for which payment has not yet been received.

Common Examples:

  • Services performed but not yet billed
  • Interest earned but not yet received
  • Rent earned but not yet collected
  • Commissions earned but not yet received

The Adjusting Entry Process:

  1. Identify Unrecorded Revenue: Determine what services have been performed or goods delivered but not yet recorded.
  2. Calculate Amount: Determine the dollar amount of revenue earned.
  3. Adjusting Entry: Debit a receivable account (asset) and credit a revenue account.
Adjusting Entry Format:
Accounts Receivable (or other receivable) .......... XXX
    Revenue Account ........................................ XXX

Effect of Adjusting Entry:

  • Increases assets (receivable)
  • Increases revenues
  • Increases equity (through net income)

Example 6: Accrued Service Revenue

Scenario: Pioneer Advertising has performed $2,000 of advertising services for a client during October, but has not yet billed the client or recorded the revenue by October 31.

Problem: Without an adjusting entry, both revenues and assets would be understated.

Adjusting Entry (October 31):

Accounts Receivable ................ 2,000
    Service Revenue ........................ 2,000
(To record revenue earned but not yet billed)

Result:

  • Assets increase by $2,000 (Accounts Receivable)
  • Revenues increase by $2,000 (Service Revenue)
  • The revenue is properly matched to October when the service was performed

Subsequent Entry (When Cash is Received in November):

Cash .......................................... 2,000
    Accounts Receivable .................. 2,000
(To record collection of accounts receivable)

Important Note: When cash is received in November, NO revenue is recorded because the revenue was already recognized in October when it was earned.

Example 7: Accrued Interest Revenue

Scenario: On November 1, a company loaned $10,000 to an employee at 6% annual interest. The loan and interest are due in 6 months (May 1). The company's year-end is December 31.

Calculate Accrued Interest (December 31):

Principal: $10,000
Annual Interest Rate: 6%
Time Period: 2 months (November and December)

Interest = Principal × Rate × Time
Interest = $10,000 × 6% × (2/12)
Interest = $10,000 × 0.06 × 0.1667
Interest = $100

Adjusting Entry (December 31):

Interest Receivable .................. 100
    Interest Revenue ........................ 100
(To record interest earned for Nov-Dec)

When Loan is Repaid (May 1, next year):

Total Interest for 6 months = $10,000 × 6% × (6/12) = $300
Interest already recorded (Nov-Dec): $100
Interest for Jan-Apr: $300 - $100 = $200

Entry on May 1 (next year):

Cash .......................................... 10,300
    Notes Receivable ........................ 10,000
    Interest Receivable ..................... 100
    Interest Revenue ........................ 200
(To record collection of note plus interest)

6.2 Accrued Expenses (Accrued Liabilities)

Definition: Accrued expenses are expenses that have been incurred but not yet paid in cash or recorded. These represent obligations that exist at the end of the accounting period.

Common Examples:

  • Salaries and wages owed to employees
  • Interest owed on loans or notes payable
  • Taxes owed but not yet paid
  • Utilities used but not yet billed
  • Rent expense incurred but not yet paid

The Adjusting Entry Process:

  1. Identify Unrecorded Expense: Determine what expenses have been incurred but not yet recorded.
  2. Calculate Amount: Determine the dollar amount of expense incurred.
  3. Adjusting Entry: Debit an expense account and credit a payable account (liability).
Adjusting Entry Format:
Expense Account ........................................ XXX
    Accounts Payable (or other payable) .......... XXX

Effect of Adjusting Entry:

  • Increases expenses
  • Increases liabilities (payable)
  • Decreases equity (through net income)

Example 8: Accrued Salaries

Scenario: Pioneer Advertising pays its employees every Friday for a 5-day work week (Monday-Friday). The weekly payroll is $10,000 ($2,000 per day). October 31 (year-end) falls on a Wednesday. Employees have worked Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday but will not be paid until Friday, November 2.

Visualization:

Mon Oct 28 Tue Oct 29 Wed Oct 30 Thu Oct 31 Fri Nov 1 Fri Nov 2
Worked Worked Worked YEAR-END Worked PAYDAY
3 days in October = $6,000 accrued 2 days in November = $4,000

Calculate Accrued Salaries (October 31):

Daily salary: $10,000 ÷ 5 days = $2,000 per day
Days worked but unpaid: 3 days (Mon, Tue, Wed)
Accrued salaries: $2,000 × 3 = $6,000

Adjusting Entry (October 31):

Salaries and Wages Expense ...... 6,000
    Salaries and Wages Payable ........ 6,000
(To record accrued salaries for Oct 28-30)

Result:

  • October expenses properly include $6,000 for work performed in October
  • October 31 balance sheet shows a liability of $6,000 owed to employees

Payment Entry (November 2):

Salaries and Wages Payable ........ 6,000
Salaries and Wages Expense ...... 4,000
    Cash .......................................... 10,000
(To record payment of salaries: $6,000 for Oct + $4,000 for Nov)

Analysis: The $10,000 payment is split: $6,000 reduces the liability from October, and $4,000 is expense for November (Thursday and Friday).

Example 9: Accrued Interest Expense

Scenario: On September 1, a company borrowed $50,000 from a bank by signing a 9% note payable. Interest and principal are due in 12 months (September 1, next year). The company's year-end is December 31.

Calculate Accrued Interest (December 31):

Principal: $50,000
Annual Interest Rate: 9%
Time Period: 4 months (September through December)

Interest = Principal × Rate × Time
Interest = $50,000 × 9% × (4/12)
Interest = $50,000 × 0.09 × 0.3333
Interest = $1,500

Adjusting Entry (December 31):

Interest Expense ...................... 1,500
    Interest Payable ........................ 1,500
(To record interest expense for Sept-Dec)

When Note is Repaid (September 1, next year):

Total Interest for 12 months = $50,000 × 9% = $4,500
Interest already recorded (Sept-Dec): $1,500
Interest for Jan-Aug: $4,500 - $1,500 = $3,000

Entry on September 1 (next year):

Notes Payable .......................... 50,000
Interest Payable ........................ 1,500
Interest Expense ...................... 3,000
    Cash .......................................... 54,500
(To record payment of note plus interest)

⚠️ Summary: Accruals vs. Deferrals

Type Cash Timing Recognition Timing Adjusting Entry Effect
Prepaid Expenses Cash paid FIRST Expense recognized LATER ↓ Asset, ↑ Expense
Unearned Revenues Cash received FIRST Revenue recognized LATER ↓ Liability, ↑ Revenue
Accrued Revenues Cash received LATER Revenue recognized FIRST ↑ Asset, ↑ Revenue
Accrued Expenses Cash paid LATER Expense recognized FIRST ↑ Expense, ↑ Liability

7. Adjusted Trial Balance and Financial Statements

7.1 The Adjusted Trial Balance

Definition: The adjusted trial balance is a list of all accounts and their balances after adjusting entries have been recorded and posted. It is prepared to prove the equality of debits and credits after adjustments.

Purpose:

  • Verify that total debits equal total credits after adjustments
  • Provide the basis for preparing financial statements
  • Ensure all adjusting entries have been properly recorded
  • Serve as a final check before preparing financial statements

Steps to Prepare Adjusted Trial Balance:

  1. Start with Unadjusted Trial Balance: List all accounts and their balances before adjustments.
  2. Record Adjusting Entries: Journalize and post all necessary adjusting entries to the ledger accounts.
  3. Calculate Adjusted Balances: For each account, add or subtract the adjusting entry amounts to/from the unadjusted balance.
  4. List Adjusted Balances: Prepare a new trial balance showing all adjusted account balances.
  5. Verify Equality: Ensure total debits equal total credits.

Example 10: Preparing an Adjusted Trial Balance

Scenario: Pioneer Advertising Agency's trial balance and adjusting entries for October 31, 2024.

  • The adjusted trial balance is now ready for financial statement preparation

7.2 Preparing Financial Statements from Adjusted Trial Balance

Financial statements are prepared directly from the adjusted trial balance in the following order:

  1. Income Statement: Prepared first using revenue and expense accounts from the adjusted trial balance. Shows the company's profitability for the period.
  2. Retained Earnings Statement: Prepared second using the beginning retained earnings balance, net income from the income statement, and dividends.
  3. Balance Sheet: Prepared last using asset, liability, and equity accounts. The ending retained earnings from the retained earnings statement is used.
  4. Statement of Cash Flows: Prepared using information from comparative balance sheets and the income statement (covered in later chapters).

Why This Order?

  • Net income from the income statement is needed for the retained earnings statement
  • Ending retained earnings from the retained earnings statement is needed for the balance sheet
  • Each statement builds on information from the previous statement

⚠️ Quality of Earnings

Quality of earnings refers to how sustainable and repeatable a company's earnings are. High-quality earnings:

  • Come from core business operations (not one-time events)
  • Are based on conservative accounting estimates
  • Are supported by strong cash flows
  • Are transparent and clearly disclosed

Adjusting entries play a crucial role in earnings quality by ensuring revenues and expenses are properly matched and reported in the correct period.

  • June 5: Performed landscaping services for $12,000 (received $7,000 cash; $5,000 on account)
  • June 10: Paid $3,000 for supplies (used $2,200 by month-end)
  • June 15: Received $15,000 advance payment for services to be performed in July
  • June 20: Performed services worth $8,000 (will be paid in July)
  • June 25: Incurred $4,500 in salary expense (will be paid July 5)
  • June 30: Paid $2,000 for June rent

Required:

  1. Calculate net income for June under cash-basis accounting
  2. Calculate net income for June under accrual-basis accounting
  3. Explain why the two methods produce different results
  4. Discuss which method provides a more accurate picture of the company's performance and why
  5. Explain why IFRS and GAAP require accrual-basis accounting

Essay Question 4: Revenue Recognition and Matching Principle

Essay Question 5: Quality of Earnings and Adjusting Entries

Scenario: You are analyzing two competing companies in the retail industry, both with December 31 year-ends:

Company A (Aggressive Accounting):

  • Recognizes revenue when orders are received, before goods are shipped
  • Capitalizes all advertising costs as prepaid expenses and amortizes them over 5 years
  • Uses a 40-year useful life for store fixtures (industry average is 10 years)
  • Delays recording accrued expenses until they are paid
  • Records minimal allowances for doubtful accounts

Company B (Conservative Accounting):

  • Recognizes revenue only when goods are delivered to customers
  • Expenses all advertising costs immediately
  • Uses a 10-year useful life for store fixtures
  • Records all accrued expenses in the proper period
  • Maintains adequate allowances for doubtful accounts based on historical data

Both companies reported net income of $10 million for 2024.

Required:

  1. Define "quality of earnings" and explain why it is important to financial statement users
  2. Analyze each of Company A's accounting practices and explain why they are problematic
  3. Explain which company's earnings are of higher quality and why
  4. Discuss how improper adjusting entries can be used to manipulate earnings
  5. Explain the role of auditors in ensuring proper adjusting entries are made
  6. Describe the potential consequences for Company A if it continues these aggressive accounting practices
  7. As an investor, which company would you prefer to invest in and why?
  8. Discuss how the matching principle and revenue recognition principle relate to earnings quality
