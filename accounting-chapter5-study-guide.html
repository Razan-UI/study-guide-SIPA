📚 Accounting for Merchandising Operations

Chapter 5 - Financial Accounting IFRS 3rd Edition

Learning Objective 1: Identify the Differences Between Service and Merchandising Companies

What is a Merchandising Company?

A merchandising company is a business that buys and sells goods (merchandise) to earn a profit. Unlike service companies that provide services, merchandising companies deal with tangible products.

Types of Merchandising Companies

1. Wholesalers

Definition: Companies that buy goods from manufacturers and sell them to retailers.

Examples:

  • Costco Wholesale
  • Metro Cash & Carry
  • Sysco Corporation (food distributor)

Characteristics:

  • Sell in large quantities
  • Do not sell directly to end consumers
  • Act as intermediaries between manufacturers and retailers

2. Retailers

Definition: Companies that sell goods directly to consumers.

Examples:

  • Walmart, Target, Carrefour
  • Amazon (online retailer)
  • Local grocery stores and boutiques

Characteristics:

  • Sell in smaller quantities
  • Deal directly with end consumers
  • May operate physical stores or online platforms

Service Companies vs. Merchandising Companies

Aspect Service Company Merchandising Company
Primary Activity Provide services Buy and sell goods
Revenue Source Service Revenue Sales Revenue
Inventory No inventory of goods Maintains inventory
Cost of Goods Sold Not applicable Major expense item
Examples Law firms, accounting firms, airlines Walmart, Amazon, grocery stores

The Operating Cycle

The operating cycle is the time it takes to go from cash to cash in producing revenue.

Service Company Operating Cycle

Steps:

  1. Cash → Pay for operating expenses
  2. Perform Services → Provide services to customers
  3. Accounts Receivable → Bill customers (if on credit)
  4. Cash → Collect payment from customers

Duration: Typically shorter (days to weeks)

Merchandising Company Operating Cycle

Steps:

  1. Cash → Purchase inventory
  2. Inventory → Store goods for sale
  3. Accounts Receivable → Sell goods (on credit)
  4. Cash → Collect payment from customers

Duration: Typically longer (weeks to months)

Key Difference: Includes an inventory stage, making the cycle longer and more complex

Income Measurement in Merchandising Companies

The income statement for a merchandising company differs from a service company by including Cost of Goods Sold.

Basic Income Statement Formula:

Sales Revenue
- Cost of Goods Sold
= Gross Profit
- Operating Expenses
= Profit (Net Income)

Key Terms Explained

Sales Revenue

The total amount earned from selling merchandise to customers during the period. Also called Sales.

Formula: Sales Revenue = Number of Units Sold × Selling Price per Unit

Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)

The total cost of merchandise sold during the period. This is the cost the company paid to acquire the goods it sold.

Formula: COGS = Beginning Inventory + Purchases - Ending Inventory

Also known as: Cost of Sales

Gross Profit

The difference between sales revenue and cost of goods sold. It represents the profit before deducting operating expenses.

Formula: Gross Profit = Sales Revenue - Cost of Goods Sold

Also known as: Gross Margin

Operating Expenses

Expenses incurred in the process of earning sales revenue. These are expenses not directly tied to the cost of inventory.

Examples:

  • Salaries and wages expense
  • Rent expense
  • Utilities expense
  • Advertising expense
  • Depreciation expense

Inventory Systems

Companies use one of two systems to account for inventory:

1. Perpetual Inventory System

Definition: A system that maintains detailed records of the cost of each inventory purchase and sale, and continuously shows the inventory on hand.

Characteristics:

  • Records are updated continuously with each purchase and sale
  • Inventory account is updated in real-time
  • Cost of Goods Sold is recorded with each sale
  • Provides up-to-date inventory balances

Advantages:

  • Better control over inventory
  • Immediate information on inventory levels
  • Helps prevent stockouts and overstocking
  • Easier to detect theft or errors

When Used:

  • Companies with computerized systems
  • Businesses selling high-value items (cars, electronics)
  • Most modern retail businesses

Journal Entries Example:

When merchandise is purchased:

Inventory XXX
Cash (or Accounts Payable) XXX

When merchandise is sold:

Cash (or Accounts Receivable) XXX
Sales Revenue XXX
(To record the sale)
 
Cost of Goods Sold XXX
Inventory XXX
(To record the cost of merchandise sold)

2. Periodic Inventory System

Definition: A system where detailed inventory records are not maintained, and the cost of goods sold is determined only at the end of an accounting period.

Characteristics:

  • Inventory records updated periodically (monthly, quarterly, annually)
  • Requires physical count to determine ending inventory
  • Cost of Goods Sold calculated at period end
  • Uses "Purchases" account instead of "Inventory" for acquisitions

Advantages:

  • Simpler recordkeeping
  • Lower cost to maintain
  • Suitable for small businesses

Disadvantages:

  • No real-time inventory information
  • Difficult to detect theft
  • Cannot determine COGS until period end

When Used:

  • Small businesses with limited resources
  • Companies selling low-value, high-volume items
  • Businesses without computerized systems

COGS Calculation:

Beginning Inventory
+ Purchases
= Cost of Goods Available for Sale
- Ending Inventory (from physical count)
= Cost of Goods Sold
Important Note: This chapter focuses primarily on the perpetual inventory system, which is the most commonly used system in modern businesses. The periodic system is covered in Appendix 5B.

Learning Objective 2: Explain the Recording of Purchases Under a Perpetual Inventory System

Overview of Purchase Transactions

When a merchandising company purchases inventory, several related transactions may occur:

  • Purchase of merchandise on account or for cash
  • Payment of freight costs
  • Return of merchandise or receiving allowances
  • Taking purchase discounts for early payment

1. Purchases of Merchandise

Purchase on Account (Credit)

Scenario: On October 1, Sauk Stereo purchases €3,800 of merchandise on account from PW Audio Supply, terms 2/10, n/30.

Analysis:

  • Inventory (Asset) increases by €3,800
  • Accounts Payable (Liability) increases by €3,800

Journal Entry:

Date Account Titles Debit Credit
Oct. 1 Inventory
Accounts Payable		 €3,800
€3,800

Explanation: Under the perpetual system, purchases of merchandise are recorded directly in the Inventory account (not a separate Purchases account).

Understanding Credit Terms

Credit Terms: 2/10, n/30

This notation means:

  • 2/10: 2% discount if paid within 10 days
  • n/30: Net amount (full amount) is due within 30 days

Other Common Terms:

  • 1/10, n/30: 1% discount if paid within 10 days, net due in 30 days
  • 2/10, n/60: 2% discount if paid within 10 days, net due in 60 days
  • n/30: No discount, full payment due in 30 days
  • n/EOM: Net due at End of Month

2. Freight Costs

Freight costs (shipping costs) are incurred when transporting goods from the seller to the buyer. The question is: Who pays?

FOB Shipping Point

Definition: "Free On Board Shipping Point" means ownership of goods passes to the buyer when the public carrier accepts the goods from the seller.

Who Pays Freight: BUYER pays the freight costs

Accounting Treatment: Buyer adds freight costs to the Inventory account

Example: On October 1, Sauk Stereo pays Acme Freight Company €150 for freight charges on merchandise purchased from PW Audio Supply.

Journal Entry (Buyer):

Date Account Titles Debit Credit
Oct. 1 Inventory
Cash		 €150
€150

Rationale: Freight costs are part of the cost of purchasing inventory, so they increase the Inventory account.

FOB Destination

Definition: "Free On Board Destination" means ownership of goods remains with the seller until the goods reach the buyer.

Who Pays Freight: SELLER pays the freight costs

Accounting Treatment: Seller records freight costs as a delivery expense (Freight-Out or Delivery Expense)

Example: If PW Audio Supply pays the €150 freight charges for delivery to Sauk Stereo.

Journal Entry (Seller):

Date Account Titles Debit Credit
Oct. 1 Freight-Out (or Delivery Expense)
Cash		 €150
€150

Rationale: For the seller, freight costs are an operating expense, not part of inventory cost.

Memory Aid:
  • FOB Shipping Point: Buyer pays freight → Add to Inventory
  • FOB Destination: Seller pays freight → Record as Expense

3. Purchase Returns and Allowances

A buyer may be dissatisfied with merchandise and either return it or request a price reduction (allowance).

Purchase Return

Definition: A return occurs when the buyer returns goods to the seller.

Scenario: On October 4, Sauk Stereo returns €300 of merchandise to PW Audio Supply.

Analysis:

  • Inventory (Asset) decreases by €300
  • Accounts Payable (Liability) decreases by €300

Journal Entry:

Date Account Titles Debit Credit
Oct. 4 Accounts Payable
Inventory		 €300
€300

Purchase Allowance

Definition: An allowance occurs when the buyer keeps the merchandise but receives a reduction in the purchase price.

Scenario: Sauk Stereo discovers some merchandise is slightly damaged and negotiates a €200 allowance from PW Audio Supply.

Journal Entry:

Date Account Titles Debit Credit
Oct. 4 Accounts Payable
Inventory		 €200
€200

Note: The accounting treatment is the same for returns and allowances - both reduce Inventory and Accounts Payable.

4. Purchase Discounts

A purchase discount is a cash discount claimed by a buyer for prompt payment of a balance due.

Taking the Discount

Scenario: On October 11, Sauk Stereo pays the balance due to PW Audio Supply, taking the 2% cash discount allowed for payment within 10 days.

Calculation:

  • Original purchase: €3,800
  • Less: Purchase return: (€300)
  • Balance due: €3,500
  • Discount (2% × €3,500): (€70)
  • Cash paid: €3,430

Analysis:

  • Accounts Payable decreases by €3,500 (full amount owed)
  • Inventory decreases by €70 (discount reduces cost of inventory)
  • Cash decreases by €3,430 (amount actually paid)

Journal Entry:

Date Account Titles Debit Credit
Oct. 11 Accounts Payable
Inventory
Cash		 €3,500
€70
€3,430

Explanation: The discount is credited to Inventory because it reduces the cost of the merchandise purchased.

Missing the Discount

Scenario: If Sauk Stereo does not pay within the discount period, it must pay the full amount of €3,500.

Journal Entry:

Date Account Titles Debit Credit
Oct. 31 Accounts Payable
Cash		 €3,500
€3,500

Why Take Purchase Discounts?

Purchase discounts represent significant savings. Missing a 2/10, n/30 discount is equivalent to paying approximately 36% annual interest!

Calculation:

  • Discount rate: 2%
  • Time saved by paying early: 20 days (30 - 10)
  • Number of 20-day periods in a year: 365 ÷ 20 = 18.25
  • Annual interest rate: 2% × 18.25 = 36.5%

Conclusion: Companies should almost always take purchase discounts, even if they need to borrow money to do so!

Summary of Purchase Transactions

Complete Example: Sauk Stereo's October Purchases

Date Transaction Effect on Inventory
Oct. 1 Purchased merchandise on account +€3,800
Oct. 1 Paid freight charges +€150
Oct. 4 Returned merchandise -€300
Oct. 11 Took purchase discount (2% × €3,500) -€70
Net Cost of Inventory €3,580

Final Inventory Balance: €3,580

This represents the actual cost of the merchandise that Sauk Stereo has available for sale.

Learning Objective 3: Explain the Recording of Sales Under a Perpetual Inventory System

Overview of Sales Transactions

When a merchandising company sells inventory, several related transactions may occur:

  • Sale of merchandise on account or for cash
  • Granting of sales returns or allowances
  • Offering sales discounts for early payment

1. Sales of Merchandise

Under the perpetual inventory system, two journal entries are required for each sale:

  1. Record the sale (revenue recognition)
  2. Record the cost of goods sold (expense recognition)

Sale on Account (Credit)

Scenario: On October 5, PW Audio Supply sells €3,800 of merchandise on account to Sauk Stereo, terms 2/10, n/30. The merchandise cost PW Audio Supply €2,400.

Entry 1: Record the Sale

Analysis:

  • Accounts Receivable (Asset) increases by €3,800
  • Sales Revenue increases by €3,800
Date Account Titles Debit Credit
Oct. 5 Accounts Receivable
Sales Revenue		 €3,800
€3,800

Entry 2: Record the Cost

Analysis:

  • Cost of Goods Sold (Expense) increases by €2,400
  • Inventory (Asset) decreases by €2,400
Date Account Titles Debit Credit
Oct. 5 Cost of Goods Sold
Inventory		 €2,400
€2,400

Result:

  • Sales Revenue: €3,800
  • Cost of Goods Sold: €2,400
  • Gross Profit: €1,400

Cash Sale

Scenario: On October 7, PW Audio Supply sells €2,200 of merchandise for cash. The merchandise cost PW Audio Supply €1,400.

Entry 1: Record the Sale

Date Account Titles Debit Credit
Oct. 7 Cash
Sales Revenue		 €2,200
€2,200

Entry 2: Record the Cost

Date Account Titles Debit Credit
Oct. 7 Cost of Goods Sold
Inventory		 €1,400
€1,400
Key Point: Under the perpetual inventory system, Cost of Goods Sold is recorded at the time of each sale, not at the end of the period. This provides continuous information about profitability.

2. Sales Returns and Allowances

Sales returns and allowances are contra revenue accounts that reduce sales revenue.

Sales Return

Definition: A sales return occurs when a customer returns merchandise to the seller.

Scenario: On October 8, Sauk Stereo returns €300 of merchandise to PW Audio Supply. The merchandise originally cost PW Audio Supply €140.

Entry 1: Record the Return (Reverse the Sale)

Analysis:

  • Sales Returns and Allowances (Contra Revenue) increases by €300
  • Accounts Receivable (Asset) decreases by €300
Date Account Titles Debit Credit
Oct. 8 Sales Returns and Allowances
Accounts Receivable		 €300
€300

Entry 2: Record the Cost (Reverse the COGS)

Analysis:

  • Inventory (Asset) increases by €140 (merchandise back in stock)
  • Cost of Goods Sold (Expense) decreases by €140
Date Account Titles Debit Credit
Oct. 8 Inventory
Cost of Goods Sold		 €140
€140

Sales Allowance

Definition: A sales allowance occurs when the seller reduces the selling price but the customer keeps the merchandise.

Scenario: PW Audio Supply grants a €200 allowance to a customer for slightly damaged merchandise.

Journal Entry:

Date Account Titles Debit Credit
Oct. 10 Sales Returns and Allowances
Accounts Receivable		 €200
€200

Note: For allowances, no second entry is needed because the merchandise stays with the customer (no change to inventory or COGS).

Why Use a Separate Account?

Question: Why not just debit Sales Revenue directly?

Answer: Using a separate "Sales Returns and Allowances" account provides important information:

  • Shows the extent of returns and allowances
  • Helps management monitor product quality issues
  • Indicates customer satisfaction levels
  • Allows comparison across periods

High returns may indicate:

  • Quality control problems
  • Shipping/handling issues
  • Inaccurate product descriptions
  • Need for better customer service

3. Sales Discounts

A sales discount is a reduction given by a seller for prompt payment of a credit sale.

Customer Takes the Discount

Scenario: On October 15, PW Audio Supply receives payment from Sauk Stereo for the October 5 sale, less the October 8 return. Sauk Stereo takes the 2% discount.

Calculation:

  • Original sale: €3,800
  • Less: Sales return: (€300)
  • Balance due: €3,500
  • Discount (2% × €3,500): (€70)
  • Cash received: €3,430

Analysis:

  • Cash increases by €3,430
  • Sales Discounts (Contra Revenue) increases by €70
  • Accounts Receivable decreases by €3,500

Journal Entry:

Date Account Titles Debit Credit
Oct. 15 Cash
Sales Discounts
Accounts Receivable		 €3,430
€70

€3,500

Customer Misses the Discount

Scenario: If Sauk Stereo pays after the discount period, PW Audio Supply receives the full €3,500.

Journal Entry:

Date Account Titles Debit Credit
Oct. 31 Cash
Accounts Receivable		 €3,500
€3,500

Sales Discounts vs. Purchase Discounts

Aspect Purchase Discount (Buyer) Sales Discount (Seller)
Account Type Reduces Inventory (Asset) Contra Revenue
Normal Balance Credit (reduces debit balance) Debit (reduces credit balance)
Effect Reduces cost of inventory Reduces sales revenue
Financial Statement Balance Sheet (Inventory) Income Statement (Net Sales)

Net Sales Calculation

Net Sales Formula:

Sales Revenue
- Sales Returns and Allowances
- Sales Discounts
= Net Sales

Example: Calculating Net Sales

PW Audio Supply's October sales data:

  • Sales Revenue: €480,000
  • Sales Returns and Allowances: €12,000
  • Sales Discounts: €8,000

Calculation:

Sales Revenue €480,000
Less: Sales Returns and Allowances (€12,000)
Less: Sales Discounts (€8,000)
Net Sales €460,000

Learning Objective 4: Explain the Steps in the Accounting Cycle for a Merchandising Company

Overview

The accounting cycle for a merchandising company is similar to that of a service company, with some key differences related to inventory and cost of goods sold.

The Accounting Cycle Steps

  1. Analyze business transactions
  2. Journalize the transactions
  3. Post to ledger accounts
  4. Prepare a trial balance
  5. Journalize and post adjusting entries
  6. Prepare an adjusted trial balance
  7. Prepare financial statements
  8. Journalize and post closing entries
  9. Prepare a post-closing trial balance

Adjusting Entries for Merchandising Companies

Merchandising companies use the same types of adjusting entries as service companies, plus some specific to inventory operations.

1. Adjusting Inventory for Shrinkage

Inventory Shrinkage: The loss of inventory due to theft, damage, or errors.

Scenario: PW Audio Supply's perpetual inventory records show €40,500 of inventory on hand. A physical count reveals only €40,000 of inventory actually exists. The difference of €500 represents shrinkage.

Analysis:

  • Cost of Goods Sold increases by €500 (shrinkage is treated as an expense)
  • Inventory decreases by €500

Adjusting Entry:

Date Account Titles Debit Credit
Oct. 31 Cost of Goods Sold
Inventory
(To adjust for inventory shrinkage)		 €500
€500

Explanation: This entry adjusts the inventory account to match the physical count and recognizes the loss as part of cost of goods sold.

2. Other Common Adjusting Entries

Merchandising companies also make standard adjusting entries:

a) Prepaid Insurance

Insurance Expense XXX
Prepaid Insurance XXX

b) Depreciation

Depreciation Expense XXX
Accumulated Depreciation XXX

c) Accrued Salaries

Salaries and Wages Expense XXX
Salaries and Wages Payable XXX

d) Unearned Revenue

Unearned Sales Revenue XXX
Sales Revenue XXX

Closing entries transfer temporary account balances to Retained Earnings. Merchandising companies close additional accounts related to sales and cost of goods sold.

Summary of Closing Entries

Accounts that are CLOSED (temporary accounts):

  • Sales Revenue
  • Sales Returns and Allowances
  • Sales Discounts
  • Cost of Goods Sold
  • All Operating Expenses
  • Dividends

Accounts that are NOT CLOSED (permanent accounts):

  • Assets (including Inventory)
  • Liabilities
  • Share Capital
  • Retained Earnings

Post-Closing Trial Balance

After closing entries are posted, prepare a post-closing trial balance to verify that:

  • Debits equal credits
  • All temporary accounts have zero balances
  • Only permanent accounts (balance sheet accounts) remain

Learning Objective 5: Distinguish Between a Multiple-Step and a Single-Step Income Statement

Overview

Companies can present their income statements in two formats:

  • Multiple-Step Income Statement: Shows several steps in determining net income
  • Single-Step Income Statement: Shows only one step (total revenues minus total expenses)

Multiple-Step Income Statement

The multiple-step income statement is more detailed and provides more useful information for analysis. It separates operating activities from non-operating activities.

Key Sections Explained

1. Net Sales

Formula: Sales Revenue - Sales Returns and Allowances - Sales Discounts

This represents the actual revenue earned from selling merchandise after deducting returns and discounts.

2. Gross Profit

Formula: Net Sales - Cost of Goods Sold

This shows the profit before deducting operating expenses. It indicates how much the company marks up its merchandise.

Gross Profit Rate:

Gross Profit Rate = (Gross Profit ÷ Net Sales) × 100%
= (€144,000 ÷ €460,000) × 100% = 31.3%

This means for every €1 of sales, the company earns €0.313 in gross profit.

3. Operating Expenses

Expenses incurred in the normal course of business operations, divided into:

a) Selling Expenses: Related to making sales and delivering merchandise

  • Sales salaries and commissions
  • Advertising
  • Freight-out (delivery costs)
  • Store rent and utilities
  • Depreciation of store equipment

b) Administrative Expenses: Related to general management and administration

  • Office salaries
  • Office rent and utilities
  • Insurance
  • Depreciation of office equipment
  • Legal and accounting fees

4. Income from Operations

Formula: Gross Profit - Operating Expenses

This shows the profit (or loss) from the company's primary business activities, excluding non-operating items.

Also called: Operating Income or Operating Profit

5. Other Revenues and Gains

Revenues and gains from activities outside the company's main operations:

  • Interest Revenue: Income from investments or bank deposits
  • Dividend Revenue: Income from owning shares in other companies
  • Rent Revenue: Income from renting out property
  • Gain on Disposal of Assets: Profit from selling equipment or other assets

6. Other Expenses and Losses

Expenses and losses from activities outside the company's main operations:

  • Interest Expense: Cost of borrowing money
  • Loss on Disposal of Assets: Loss from selling equipment or other assets
  • Casualty Losses: Losses from theft, fire, or natural disasters

Single-Step Income Statement

The single-step income statement is simpler and lists all revenues together and all expenses together, with one calculation to arrive at net income.

PW Audio Supply
Income Statement
For the Year Ended October 31, 2025

Comparison: Multiple-Step vs. Single-Step

Aspect Multiple-Step Single-Step
Complexity More detailed and complex Simpler and easier to prepare
Subtotals Shows gross profit, operating income, etc. No intermediate subtotals
Operating vs. Non-Operating Clearly separates operating from non-operating Does not distinguish
Analysis Provides more information for analysis Limited analytical information
Usage Preferred by most companies Used by some service companies
Net Income Same final result Same final result
Important Note: Both formats arrive at the same net income figure. The difference is in the level of detail and the intermediate calculations shown. Most merchandising companies prefer the multiple-step format because it provides more useful information for decision-making and analysis.

Statement of Comprehensive Income

Under IFRS, companies may also report other comprehensive income items that are not included in net income but affect equity.

Examples of Other Comprehensive Income:

  • Unrealized gains/losses on certain investments
  • Foreign currency translation adjustments
  • Certain pension adjustments

Format:

Profit (Net Income) €XXX
Other Comprehensive Income (net of tax) €XXX
Total Comprehensive Income €XXX

Case Study 4: Inventory Shrinkage at SuperMart

Scenario: SuperMart uses a perpetual inventory system. On December 31, 2025, the company's accounting records show inventory of €125,000. A physical count reveals actual inventory of €121,500. The company also needs to make the following year-end adjusting entries:

  • Prepaid insurance of €6,000 has expired
  • Depreciation on store equipment is €15,000
  • Accrued salaries payable amount to €4,200

Question: Prepare all necessary adjusting entries for SuperMart on December 31, 2025, and explain the impact of inventory shrinkage.

Solution:

1. Adjusting Entry for Inventory Shrinkage:

Shrinkage = €125,000 - €121,500 = €3,500

Cost of Goods Sold€3,500
Inventory€3,500
(To adjust inventory for shrinkage)

2. Adjusting Entry for Expired Insurance:

Insurance Expense€6,000
Prepaid Insurance€6,000
(To record expired insurance)

3. Adjusting Entry for Depreciation:

Depreciation Expense—Store Equipment€15,000
Accumulated Depreciation—Store Equipment€15,000
(To record depreciation on store equipment)

4. Adjusting Entry for Accrued Salaries:

Salaries and Wages Expense€4,200
Salaries and Wages Payable€4,200
(To record accrued salaries)

Impact of Inventory Shrinkage:

  • Causes: Theft, damage, spoilage, or recording errors
  • Financial Impact: Increases Cost of Goods Sold by €3,500, reducing net income
  • Balance Sheet: Reduces Inventory from €125,000 to €121,500
  • Management Action: High shrinkage may indicate need for better security, inventory controls, or employee training

Shrinkage Rate: (€3,500 ÷ €125,000) × 100% = 2.8%

This means 2.8% of inventory was lost due to shrinkage, which management should investigate.
